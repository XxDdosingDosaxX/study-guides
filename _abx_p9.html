Who needs IV antibiotics

📋

The route decision is separate from the admission decision

Residents routinely conflate two different questions. "Does this patient need to be in hospital?" is about physiology, monitoring, and social support. "Does this patient need intravenous antibiotics?" is about absorption and achievable concentration at the site of infection. Plenty of admitted patients should be on oral drugs, and a few outpatients genuinely need parenteral therapy.

IV is genuinely requiredWhy
Haemodynamic instability / septic shockSplanchnic hypoperfusion makes enteral absorption unpredictable, and the volume of distribution is deranged.
Cannot swallow, vomiting, ileus, malabsorption, NPO, NG suctionNo absorption, no drug.
Meningitis and other CNS infectionRequires bactericidal CSF concentrations that oral agents cannot reliably produce.
Endocarditis (at least the initial phase)Bacteria in an avascular vegetation need sustained high bactericidal levels.
S. aureus bacteraemiaHigh complication and metastatic-seeding rate; needs source hunting, repeat cultures and ID involvement.
Profound neutropeniaMucositis makes absorption erratic and the consequences of failure are severe.
No adequate oral option exists for the organismE.g. Pseudomonas outside ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin susceptibility; most ESBL and CRE.
Prosthetic material, undrained collection, or uncontrolled sourceThe route is not the problem — the source is. Drain it.
Being on a hospital ward is not, by itself, an indication for intravenous antibiotics. If the patient is stable, improving, and eating, and a bioavailable oral agent covers the organism, the IV line is adding phlebitis, thrombosis and line-infection risk with no benefit. Across the oral-versus-IV literature in stable patients, no randomised trial has shown intravenous administration superior to an equivalent oral agent.

Diabetic foot infection (IWGDF/IDSA)

Diabetes changes the microbiology as well as the severity. Acute, previously untreated, mild infection is still mostly gram-positive and can be treated narrowly. Chronic, previously treated, ischaemic, deep or severe infection is polymicrobial — aerobic gram-positives, Enterobacterales and anaerobes together — and needs broader cover. Always probe to bone: a positive probe-to-bone test in a patient with a high pre-test probability substantially raises the likelihood of osteomyelitis and changes the duration from days to weeks.

Necrotising soft-tissue infection — a surgical emergency

Antibiotics do not treat this disease; the operating room does. Mortality rises with every hour of delay to debridement. Do not let imaging, a score, or a consultant's availability delay surgical consultation. Give antibiotics on the way to theatre.

Hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia (IDSA/ATS 2016)

HAP = pneumonia occurring ≥48 h after admission and not incubating at admission. VAP = pneumonia occurring >48 h after intubation. Cover MSSA, Pseudomonas and other gram-negative bacilli in everyone; add MRSA and a second antipseudomonal agent by risk.

Seven days is the answer for HAP/VAP — equivalent outcomes to 15 days with less resistance emergence, and the 2016 guideline extends this even to non-fermenting gram-negative rods. Aspiration. Most aspiration pneumonia does not need routine anaerobic coverage — ceftriaxone or ampicillin-sulbactam is adequate. Add dedicated anaerobic cover (metronidazole, or use ampicillin-sulbactam/pip-tazo which already have it) only for lung abscess, necrotising pneumonia, empyema, or severe periodontal disease. Aspiration pneumonitis — a chemical injury within hours of a witnessed aspiration, often afebrile with rapid radiographic improvement — needs no antibiotics at all.

Pyelonephritis not improving by 48–72 h needs imaging, not a broader antibiotic. You are looking for obstruction, a perinephric or renal abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis, or a stone. An obstructed infected system is a urological emergency requiring decompression — nephrostomy or stent — and no antibiotic regimen substitutes for it.
Do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in the hospital either. A positive urine culture in a patient with delirium and no urinary symptoms is one of the commonest sources of unnecessary inpatient antibiotics. Look for another cause of the delirium first. The exceptions remain pregnancy and pre-urologic-procedure.

Complicated intra-abdominal infection

Every intra-abdominal regimen must cover gram-negative Enterobacterales plus anaerobes. Either use a single agent that does both (pip-tazo, a carbapenem, a cephamycin) or combine a cephalosporin/fluoroquinolone with metronidazole. Adding metronidazole to pip-tazo or a carbapenem is redundant. Source control — drainage, resection, diversion — outranks every antibiotic decision here.

Timing of biliary drainage in cholangitis. Grade I (mild) usually responds to antibiotics alone — drain if there is no response within 24 h. Grade II (moderate) needs early endoscopic or percutaneous drainage; in the international validation cohort, drainage within 24 h was associated with lower 30-day mortality than later or no drainage (1.7% vs 3.4%). Grade III (severe) needs organ support first, then drainage as soon as the patient can tolerate it.

Bacterial meningitis — the most time-critical antibiotic decision in medicine

Give dexamethasone plus antibiotics immediately. If a CT is needed before LP, draw blood cultures, give dexamethasone and antibiotics first, and do the LP afterwards — CSF cell counts, protein and glucose remain interpretable for hours, and Gram stain and PCR often still yield the organism.

Oral therapy works for bone and joint infection. The OVIVA trial randomised 1,054 patients to oral versus IV therapy for the first 6 weeks of bone and joint infection and found oral non-inferior (treatment failure 13.2% oral vs 14.6% IV), with far fewer catheter complications (1.0% vs 9.4%) and a shorter stay. This was ID-supervised with highly bioavailable oral regimens — it is a specialist decision, not a reflexive floor switch, but it has ended the automatic "6 weeks of IV" reflex.

Surgical indications — do not delay the surgical conversation: heart failure from valvular dysfunction; uncontrolled infection (abscess, fistula, enlarging vegetation on therapy); persistent bacteraemia beyond 5–7 days of appropriate therapy; prosthetic valve endocarditis; fungal or highly resistant organisms; recurrent emboli or a large mobile vegetation (>10 mm) with prior embolism.
POET randomised 400 patients with stabilised left-sided endocarditis, after at least 10 days of IV therapy, to partial oral versus continued IV therapy and found the oral strategy non-inferior (composite event 9.0% vs 12.1%), with post-randomisation stay of 3 days versus 19. It is an ID-directed decision in a highly selected, stabilised patient — not a routine floor manoeuvre.
Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia is never a "contaminant" and never a short course. Every case needs: repeat blood cultures every 48–72 h until clearance, echocardiography (TOE if prosthetic material, persistent bacteraemia, or a high-risk picture), a source hunt for metastatic foci, removal of any infected line, and ID consultation — which is independently associated with lower mortality. Minimum 14 days from the first negative culture for genuinely uncomplicated disease; 4–6 weeks if complicated.
