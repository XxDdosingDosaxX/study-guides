Heart Failure. A clinical reference across three settings — clinic, wards, and the ICU. Let's build this up so the drugs actually make sense, not just memorize a list.

First, the one-liner. Heart failure is a clinical syndrome where the heart can't deliver enough output, or can only do it at the cost of high filling pressures. We confirm it with elevated natriuretic peptides and objective congestion. We split it by ejection fraction: reduced, or HFrEF, is an EF of forty percent or less. Mildly reduced is forty-one to forty-nine. Preserved, HFpEF, is fifty percent or more.

Now the key idea that ties everything together — the neurohormonal cascade. Whatever the original insult, a big heart attack, years of hypertension, a leaky valve, a cardiomyopathy, the endpoint is a heart that can't move enough blood forward. The body senses that drop in effective arterial volume exactly like it senses bleeding, and it turns on two ancient rescue systems: the sympathetic nervous system, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, RAAS. For minutes, that's lifesaving. Over months, it IS the disease. Think of it like flooring the gas pedal on an engine that's already overheating. More adrenaline, more angiotensin, buys a little speed but burns the engine out faster. Every heart failure drug is essentially easing off that pedal.

So here's the payoff. The four pillars of guideline-directed medical therapy each cut a different wire in that circuit. Number one, an ARNI, sacubitril-valsartan, blocks angiotensin and preserves the protective natriuretic peptides by inhibiting neprilysin. Number two, a beta-blocker, silences the toxic sympathetic drive and actually raises the ejection fraction over months. Number three, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, an MRA like spironolactone, blocks aldosterone, which cuts both fluid retention AND myocardial fibrosis. Number four, an SGLT2 inhibitor, dapagliflozin or empagliflozin, unloads volume and improves the heart's energetics, and it works whether or not the patient has diabetes. The modern approach is to get all four on board early, at low doses, then titrate up. Low-dose quadruple therapy beats high-dose partial therapy — that's the STRONG-HF lesson.

A few dosing pearls. ARNI needs a thirty-six hour washout from an ACE inhibitor to avoid angioedema, but no washout from an ARB. Start an MRA only if the eGFR is above thirty and potassium is below five, recheck labs around day three and day seven, and stop it if you can't keep potassium under five-point-five. Only three beta-blockers have mortality benefit: carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, and bisoprolol. And if a patient gets the classic ACE-inhibitor cough, that's bradykinin — switch them to an ARB.

Now let's move to the clinic context. Outpatient, this is the slow burn: exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, waking up short of breath, fatigue, leg swelling, a creeping weight gain. The most specific findings are an S3 gallop and an elevated JVP. When you suspect it, your first tests are a natriuretic peptide and an echocardiogram — the echo gives you the EF, which decides the whole phenotype. Also get an ECG, a basic metabolic panel, CBC, thyroid, and iron studies, because iron deficiency is common and treatable even without anemia. And remember, oral iron doesn't work in heart failure — use IV iron.

For HFpEF, the preserved-EF patients, it's a different disease. The problem is a stiff ventricle driven by inflammation from hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and aging — not systolic remodeling. That's why the classic HFrEF drugs mostly failed here. The one class that clearly works is the SGLT2 inhibitor, now a Class 1 recommendation, with finerenone emerging as a second option. Think of HFrEF as a weak pump and HFpEF as a stiff balloon — you can't squeeze harder into a stiff balloon, so you manage the volume, the blood pressure, the heart rate, and the atrial kick.

The clinic is also where prevention lives. Know the USPSTF Grade A and B recommendations for your cardiac patients: Grade A for blood pressure screening in all adults and for tobacco cessation; Grade B for a statin in adults forty to seventy-five with a risk factor and a ten-year risk over ten percent, for diabetes screening in overweight adults thirty-five to seventy, and a one-time AAA ultrasound in men sixty-five to seventy-five who ever smoked. Vaccinate against flu, pneumococcus, COVID, and RSV. And after starting an ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI, or MRA, recheck the metabolic panel in one to two weeks. One more pearl: once a patient is on sacubitril-valsartan, follow NT-proBNP, not BNP, because neprilysin inhibition falsely raises the BNP.

Now the wards — acute decompensated heart failure. First question: why now? There's almost always a trigger. Use FAILURE: forgot meds or diet, arrhythmia or anemia, ischemia or infection, lifestyle sodium and fluid, upregulation like thyrotoxicosis, renal failure, and embolism. Then classify with two questions that drive every decision: is the patient wet or dry — congested or not — and warm or cold — perfused or not. Warm and wet is the most common presentation, and you diurese them. Cold and wet is the worst, and those patients need an inotrope plus diuresis — that's ICU territory.

The workhorse on the floor is IV loop diuresis. The DOSE trial taught us two things: bolus and continuous infusion are equivalent, and high-dose beats low-dose. Practically, give IV furosemide at about two-and-a-half times the total daily home oral dose, then titrate to urine output and weight. If they're diuretic resistant, add a thiazide like metolazone before the loop for sequential nephron blockade, and watch the potassium and magnesium. For hypertensive flash pulmonary edema — SCAPE — reach for IV nitroglycerin, because there the problem is afterload more than total fluid. And a key pearl: a transient bump in creatinine during effective decongestion is acceptable. Don't reflexively stop diuresing a still-congested, stable patient.

Don't hold all the home meds out of reflex. Continue the beta-blocker unless the patient is cold or in shock — just reduce the dose; abrupt withdrawal worsens outcomes. Continue the ACE inhibitor or ARB unless there's hypotension, AKI, or hyperkalemia. And optimize the GDMT before discharge — the hospitalization is your highest-yield window.

When do you call the ICU? Cardiogenic shock, respiratory failure needing noninvasive ventilation or intubation, refractory pulmonary edema, malignant arrhythmia, or worsening end-organ function. And discharge disposition matters: home, home health, a skilled nursing facility for subacute rehab, acute inpatient rehab if they can tolerate three hours of therapy a day, an LTAC for prolonged complex needs like ventilator weaning, or hospice for advanced refractory disease. The readmission-prevention trifecta is a seven-day follow-up, rapid up-titration, and cardiac rehab.

Now the ICU — cardiogenic shock and the drips. Shock is a systolic blood pressure under ninety with end-organ hypoperfusion and adequate filling pressures — a cardiac index under two-point-two and a wedge over fifteen. Stage it with the SCAI system, A through E: A is at risk, B is hypotension without hypoperfusion, C is classic shock needing support, D is deteriorating, and E is extremis. Mortality climbs steeply from C to E.

The pressors. Split it into two questions: is the pressure too low, needing vasoconstriction, or is the output too low, needing inotropy. Norepinephrine is your first-line pressor in cardiogenic shock — the SOAP II trial showed dopamine caused more arrhythmias and worse mortality. For inotropy, dobutamine is first-line when the pressure tolerates it. Choose milrinone instead when the patient is on a chronic beta-blocker, because milrinone is a PDE-3 inhibitor that bypasses the blocked beta-receptor, or when there's right ventricular failure or pulmonary hypertension — but remember milrinone is renally cleared and more hypotensive. Epinephrine is a last resort, and vasopressin is a fixed-dose adjunct.

For breathing, start with noninvasive ventilation — CPAP or BiPAP — for cardiogenic pulmonary edema. The positive pressure recruits flooded alveoli and also drops preload and afterload, and it cuts the intubation rate. Intubate if that fails, if they can't protect the airway, or if they're arresting. Initial vent settings: volume assist-control, tidal volume six to eight per kilo of ideal body weight, PEEP five to ten, FiO2 to keep the sat above ninety-two. And the danger zone — around intubation, sedation plus positive pressure can crater the blood pressure in a shock patient, so have pressors ready at the bedside and use a stable induction agent like etomidate or ketamine.

For mechanical support: the balloon pump, IABP, is not routine — it's Class 3, no mortality benefit. The Impella showed a real mortality benefit in STEMI shock in the DanGer Shock trial, but with real bleeding and limb complications, so patient selection matters. VA-ECMO is reserved for refractory shock at experienced centers. Downgrade the patient once they're off or weaning pressors with a clearing lactate, breathing on their own, and stable on an oral regimen.

Let me leave you with the highest-yield takeaways. One: get all four GDMT pillars on early — don't max one before adding the next. Two: norepinephrine is your first-line pressor in shock, dobutamine or milrinone for inotropy. Three: IV loop diuretic at two-and-a-half times the home oral dose. Four: noninvasive ventilation early for flash pulmonary edema. Five: continue the beta-blocker unless the patient is cold. And six: the seven-day follow-up with rapid up-titration is the single best thing you can do to keep them out of the hospital. That's heart failure across clinic, wards, and the ICU.
