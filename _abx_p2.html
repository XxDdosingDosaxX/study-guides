Five targets — every antibiotic you will ever prescribe hits one of them

A bacterium is a pressurised bag. Inside, osmotic pressure runs several atmospheres above the outside world; the only thing preventing the cell from bursting is a rigid mesh of cross-linked sugar–peptide polymer called peptidoglycan. Human cells have no peptidoglycan, no bacterial ribosome, and no bacterial folate-synthesis pathway — and that difference is the entire basis of selective toxicity. Every antibiotic class is an attack on a structure the bacterium has and we do not.

There are only five places to attack, and knowing which one a drug hits tells you almost everything else about it — whether it kills or merely stalls, whether it works inside a cell, whether it penetrates the CSF, and what it will do to your patient's kidneys, marrow, or QT interval.

a walled city under siege. β-lactams and vancomycin stop the masons repairing the wall, so the wall fails under its own internal pressure. Daptomycin and polymyxins punch holes in the gate itself. Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, clindamycin and linezolid silence the city's factories (ribosomes) so nothing gets rebuilt. Fluoroquinolones and metronidazole shred the blueprints (DNA). TMP-SMX starves the city by cutting off the supply of raw material (folate → thymidine) it needs to copy those blueprints. Cutting the water supply (bacteriostatic) is enough when the defenders are healthy; when the immune system is absent — neutropenia, endocarditis vegetations, meningitis, prosthetic material — you must burn the city down (bactericidal).

The five druggable targets. Locate a drug on this map and you can predict its behaviour: wall-active agents need actively dividing bacteria, ribosome agents get into cells (atypicals), and only lipophilic small molecules cross an uninflamed blood–brain barrier.
Peptidoglycan. Alternating NAG–NAM sugar strands cross-linked by short peptides ending in D-Ala-D-Ala. That terminal D-Ala-D-Ala is the bullseye: β-lactams mimic it, vancomycin binds it.

The transpeptidase (PBP) reaction that β-lactams block — the cross-link that converts a loose mesh into a pressure vessel. Block it and the cell lyses.
__CREDIT_transpeptidase__
The β-lactam ring — the strained four-membered amide ring shared by penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems and aztreonam. Its ring strain is what makes it reactive enough to acylate the PBP active site, and also what makes it a target for β-lactamases.
__CREDIT_beta_lactam_structure__
Where β-lactamase cuts. The enzyme hydrolyses the same ring, opening it and destroying the D-Ala-D-Ala mimicry. β-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanate, tazobactam, avibactam) are decoys that occupy this enzyme instead.

Cidal vs static matters in exactly four situations. For most infections in an immunocompetent host, a bacteriostatic drug plus a working immune system cures the patient just as well. But when the host cannot finish the job you must kill: (1) endocarditis — bacteria sit in an avascular fibrin vegetation neutrophils cannot enter; (2) meningitis — the CSF is a relatively immunoprivileged, opsonin-poor space; (3) profound neutropenia (ANC <500); (4) osteomyelitis / prosthetic material biofilm. In those settings pick a bactericidal agent and dose it aggressively.

The single most useful PK/PD fact on the wards: β-lactams are time-dependent. If a patient with severe sepsis is failing pip-tazo, the answer is usually extended-infusion dosing (4.5 g over 4 hours q8h) rather than a bigger single dose. Conversely, if a patient is failing an aminoglycoside, the peak was too low — give a bigger dose, less often.

How bacteria fight back — the four resistance mechanisms

Resistance is not something antibiotics create; it is something they select for. Every course of antibiotics kills the susceptible flora and leaves the resistant survivors with an empty niche to repopulate. That is the entire biological argument for stewardship, and it is why the correct answer to "should I broaden?" is so often "no — I should narrow."

Gram-negatives have an extra outer membrane, which is why they can combine mechanisms 1, 3 and 4 and become pan-resistant — and why gram-negative resistance is the harder clinical problem.
Why gram-negatives are harder. A gram-positive organism has a thick but naked peptidoglycan wall — the drug only has to get through the mesh. A gram-negative has a thin wall wrapped in an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane whose only doors are porin channels, plus a periplasmic space where β-lactamases sit concentrated exactly where the drug must pass, plus efflux pumps. Vancomycin fails against gram-negatives for a purely mechanical reason: the molecule is too large to fit through the porins.
