Assess

Recognizing it — and who is on the road to it

🔍

Cardiogenic shock is not an outpatient disease — but it starts as one

Nobody manages established cardiogenic shock in a clinic. So the clinic job is genuinely three different jobs, and it's worth being explicit about which one you're doing:

  1. Identify the patient who is going to be in shock next month, and change that trajectory with guideline therapy and by fixing precipitants.
  2. Recognise the patient who is in early shock right now — sitting in your exam room, looking "a bit off," already SCAI Stage B or C — and get them to the right hospital rather than the nearest one.
  3. Resist the temptation to temporise. Nothing you can do in clinic treats this. The only intervention that helps is a fast, well-directed transfer.

The patient in front of you who is heading for shock

Risk markerWhy it predicts cardiogenic shock
Large prior anterior MI / EF ≤30%Contractile reserve is already spent. Any new insult — an arrhythmia, a pneumonia, a missed dose — pushes them off the flat part of the Starling curve with no margin.
Escalating loop-diuretic requirementThe dose creeping from 40 → 80 → 160 mg means diuretic resistance and rising neurohormonal drive. This is the most reliable outpatient signal of a patient losing compensation.
Falling blood pressure in a chronic HF patientCounterintuitive but crucial: as HF advances, blood pressure falls. A patient whose SBP has drifted from 130 to 95 and whose GDMT you keep having to cut back is declaring low output — not "good control."
GDMT intoleranceBeing unable to tolerate an ACEi/ARNI or β-blocker because of hypotension is a marker of advanced disease, not merely a pharmacological nuisance. It identifies people with no haemodynamic reserve.
Rising creatinine + hyponatraemiaCardiorenal physiology and non-osmotic ADH release — both track low effective arterial blood volume. Hyponatraemia is one of the strongest outpatient predictors of HF mortality.
Recurrent HF hospitalisationsEach admission marks a step down a staircase. Two or more in a year should trigger an advanced-HF referral — before the shock, when LVAD/transplant options are still open.
Cardiac cachexia, NYHA IV at rest, inotrope dependenceStage D HF. These patients are living in SCAI Stage A/B; the conversation about goals, devices, and transplant candidacy needs to happen now, in clinic, while they can participate in it.
Severe untreated valve diseaseCritical AS or severe MR is a mechanical bomb on a timer. The first presentation can be shock.
The most useful and most missed outpatient sign is a blood pressure that is quietly falling in a heart failure patient while you are cutting back their GDMT to accommodate it. Everyone documents it; almost nobody flags it. That trend line is the patient telling you their cardiac output is failing, months before the crash. It is the moment to refer to advanced HF — not after the first ICU admission.

The patient who is in shock right now, in your exam room

Early cardiogenic shock in clinic does not look like a code. It looks like a tired, slightly confused patient whose hands are cold and whose blood pressure is lower than usual. The 2025 ACC guidance frames recognition with the SUSPECT CS approach — pair the clinical assessment for congestion and hypoperfusion with objective labs, rather than waiting for a number to declare itself.

What you'd notice in clinicWhat it actually means
Cold, mottled, clammy hands in a warm roomα₁-mediated cutaneous shunting — the body is already sacrificing skin to defend the brain. The single highest-yield clinic sign. Shake your patient's hand.
"They're just not themselves" / mild confusionCerebral perfusion has dropped below the autoregulatory floor. This is organ failure, and family notice it before any monitor does.
SBP 30 mmHg below their usual, even if >90An explicit SCAI Stage B criterion: a >30 mmHg drop from baseline counts, whatever the absolute number. A "normal" 110 in someone who lives at 150 is hypotension.
Narrow pulse pressure; thready pulseLow stroke volume. Free, instant, and unfakeable.
Resting tachycardia, especially newThe baroreflex defending output. Its absence in a β-blocked patient is falsely reassuring — they can be profoundly shocked at a heart rate of 70.
Dropping urine output / "haven't passed water since this morning"Renal hypoperfusion plus venous congestion. A SCAI Stage C criterion at <30 mL/h.
If you suspect cardiogenic shock in clinic: call 911 now, lie them flat only if they tolerate it, give oxygen if hypoxic, get IV access and an ECG if you have them — and do nothing else. Do not give a fluid bolus "to see." Do not send them home for outpatient labs. Do not arrange a next-day echo. Do not let them drive or be driven by family. Every minute of SCAI Stage C that progresses to Stage D roughly triples in-hospital mortality (12.4% → 40.4%). The transfer is the treatment.

Preventing the shock — GDMT for the at-risk patient

💊

The four pillars — the only outpatient therapy that changes this trajectory

start all four early, then titrate

Once a patient is in cardiogenic shock, we have exactly one therapy with a durable mortality benefit (revascularisation in AMI-CS) and one device with a positive trial in a narrow population. That is a thin hand to play. By far the most effective cardiogenic shock treatment is the guideline therapy you started two years earlier — it is the reason the ventricle still has reserve when the insult lands.

look back at the spiral: Loop 3 is the neurohormonal arm — sympathetic and RAAS activation raising afterload and retaining fluid. The four pillars are, precisely, chronic blockade of that loop. That is why they work: they stop the compensations from remodelling the ventricle into a thin-walled, high-wall-stress sphere with no reserve. GDMT is Loop 3 prophylaxis.

Advanced HF — refer before the shock, not after
The referral you make in clinic decides what options exist in the ICU

By the time a patient is in SCAI Stage D on two pressors, the question "are they an LVAD or transplant candidate?" is being answered by a team who have never met them, using a chart, at 3am. That evaluation belongs in clinic, months earlier. Referral triggers — the I-NEED-HELP mnemonic:

And have the goals-of-care conversation here too — a patient who would not want an LVAD should have that documented long before they are intubated and unable to say so.

SCAI SHOCK stage — stage it, then act on it

🧮

SCAI SHOCK stage classifier (2022 Expert Consensus Update)

works at any level of care

SCAI staging deliberately needs no invasive data — it was designed so a paramedic, a clinic physician, and a transplant surgeon can all use it and mean the same thing. The 2022 update notes that a simplified classification relying on physical examination alone is enough to triage patients toward shock centres, in the same way STEMI systems route patients today. Answer the four questions the way the patient is right now.

1 · Blood pressure / heart rate
Normotensive — SBP ≥100 or at baseline
SBP <90, MAP <60, >30 mmHg drop from baseline, or HR ≥100
Profound hypotension despite maximal support
2 · Perfusion / biochemistry
Perfusion intact — normal lactate, warm, making urine
Hypoperfusion — lactate ≥2 mmol/L, UOP <30 mL/h, cool/clammy, AMS, Cr ↑1.5× or ↑LFTs
Worsening / lactate rising and persistently >2 despite initial therapy
Lactate ≥8 mmol/L, pH <7.2, or base deficit >10 mEq/L
3 · Therapy required
None beyond volume assessment
One intervention (a vasoactive drug or MCS) beyond volume resuscitation
Escalating doses, ≥2 vasoactive agents, or an MCS device added because the first therapy failed
Perfusion cannot be restored despite multiple drugs/devices; bolus pressors needed
4 · Collapse / arrest
Awake, no arrest
Unconscious, near-pulseless, cardiac collapse, or ongoing CPR
A-modifier (2022 definition)
Post-cardiac-arrest and not following commands / GCS <9
A
Two things people get wrong. First: the A-modifier changed in 2022. A single defibrillation for a brief VF arrest with normal neurology no longer earns it — the modifier now means potential anoxic brain injury (GCS <9 or not following commands). Second: staging once is nearly useless. The 2022 update is explicit that re-staging at intervals is where the value lives — improving by even one stage is a powerful favourable signal, and a stage that holds or rises is a potent negative one. Stage them on arrival, after your first intervention, and every time the picture shifts.

Prevent

USPSTF A/B & whole-patient prevention

🛡️

Grade A & B screening for the patient at cardiovascular risk

Cardiogenic shock is mostly the end of a long atherosclerotic story. The interventions that prevent it best are unglamorous and they happen decades upstream.

GradeRecommendationPopulation & how
AHypertension screening (2021)All adults ≥18 with office BP measurement. Confirm with out-of-office readings (ABPM or home BP) before starting treatment. Hypertension is the dominant driver of both the HFpEF and post-MI roads into shock.
ATobacco cessation (2021)Ask every adult about tobacco; advise cessation; offer behavioural intervention plus FDA-approved pharmacotherapy (non-pregnant adults). The single highest-yield modifiable MI risk factor.
BStatin for primary prevention (2022)Adults 40–75 with no CVD history, ≥1 risk factor (dyslipidaemia, diabetes, hypertension, smoking) and 10-year CVD risk ≥10%. (Grade C — selectively offer — at 7.5% to <10%.)
BPrediabetes & type 2 diabetes screening (2021)Adults 35–70 with overweight/obesity (BMI ≥25; consider ≥23 in Asian American patients). Refer those with prediabetes to preventive intervention. Diabetes both accelerates atherosclerosis and masks the chest pain that would have warned you.
BAAA screening (2019)One-time ultrasound in men 65–75 who have ever smoked. (Grade C — selectively offer — in men 65–75 who never smoked.) Same atherosclerotic substrate.

Beyond USPSTF — the cardiac-specific prevention that matters

  • Influenza vaccination annually. Influenza is a well-described trigger of MI and of HF decompensation; vaccination reduces cardiovascular events in patients with established heart disease. This is cardiology, not just primary care.
  • COVID-19 and pneumococcal vaccination per current schedules — same logic: infection is the precipitant that tips a marginal ventricle.
  • Cardiac rehabilitation after MI or HF hospitalisation — improves functional capacity and reduces readmission, and is dramatically under-referred.
  • Deprescribe the things that cause decompensation: NSAIDs, thiazolidinediones, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and most antiarrhythmics in HFrEF (see the drugs-to-avoid callout in Wards).
  • Sodium and fluid counselling with a written action plan, plus daily weights — the cheapest readmission prevention that exists.
Escalate

When clinic isn't enough

🚑

Red flags → ED, and which ED

Call 911 from the room — do not send them to the ED by car, and do not "arrange admission" through the office:
  • SBP <90, or >30 mmHg below their documented baseline, with any hypoperfusion sign
  • Cool, mottled extremities — with or without a low blood pressure
  • New confusion, agitation or somnolence in a cardiac patient
  • Chest pain with hypotension, or any ST elevation / new LBBB on ECG
  • Oliguria/anuria plus dyspnoea
  • Syncope in a patient with known structural heart disease or an EF ≤35%
  • A new murmur in anyone with a recent MI
  • Resting hypoxia with orthopnoea and rales
  • Lactate ≥2 if you have a point-of-care meter
Where you send them matters as much as how fast. SCAI's proposed hub-and-spoke model has spoke centres managing most Stage C patients but triggering referral on progression to Stage D — before Stage E develops. From clinic, that logic means: if you suspect cardiogenic shock, ask EMS for a PCI-capable centre with a shock team, and say the words "I think this is cardiogenic shock" on the phone. A community ED without a cath lab has to re-transfer them, and that transfer costs hours the myocardium doesn't have. If they have ST elevation, this is a cath-lab activation from your clinic, not a referral.
The hardest version of this is the chronic HF patient who looks like they always look. They are dyspnoeic, oedematous and tired at every visit — so the day they're actually in Stage C, nothing seems new. Two things protect you: check the blood pressure against their own trend (not against 120/80), and touch their hands. Adaptation to chronic low output means these patients under-declare their severity right up until they don't.
