The classes — dose, mechanism, monitoring

💊

Every agent you will order, with its adult dose in normal renal function

Mechanism. All β-lactams are structural mimics of the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of the peptidoglycan pentapeptide. They acylate the active site of the penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases) that cross-link the wall, so the cell keeps making new peptidoglycan strands but can no longer stitch them together. Autolysins continue chewing the old wall, the mesh fails, and the cell lyses under its own osmotic pressure. This only works on actively dividing bacteria — which is why β-lactams are useless against dormant organisms and why they are time-dependent killers.

β-lactam efficacy is driven by %fT>MIC — the percentage of the dosing interval that free drug stays above the MIC (roughly 40–50% for penicillins/carbapenems, 60–70% for cephalosporins, and higher in critical illness). Raising a single dose barely helps; shortening the interval or extending the infusion is what fixes an under-treated β-lactam.

The MRSA problem. Methicillin resistance is not a β-lactamase — it is the mecA gene encoding an alternative transpeptidase, PBP2a, whose active site has such low affinity for β-lactams that the cell keeps building its wall while every penicillin and cephalosporin is present. That is why adding a β-lactamase inhibitor does nothing, and why only four kinds of drug work: glycopeptides, the lipopeptide, the oxazolidinones, and the one cephalosporin engineered to fit PBP2a.

Aminoglycosides

Aminoglycosides bind irreversibly to the 30S subunit, causing mRNA misreading and membrane damage — one of the few bactericidal protein-synthesis inhibitors. Uptake across the bacterial membrane is oxygen-dependent, which explains three clinical facts at once: no anaerobic activity, poor activity in the acidic hypoxic environment of an abscess, and poor activity in lung tissue.

Why once-daily beats three-times-daily. Aminoglycoside killing is concentration-dependent (peak/MIC ≥8–10), and they have a long post-antibiotic effect, so a big peak works better. Toxicity, by contrast, is saturable uptake into renal tubular and cochlear hair cells — which means it depends on how long the concentration stays high, not how high it gets. One large daily dose therefore maximises efficacy and minimises toxicity simultaneously. The exception is synergy dosing in endocarditis, where you want continuous low-level exposure alongside a cell-wall agent and use small divided doses.
Aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity is typically non-oliguric and appears after 5–7 days; ototoxicity is often irreversible and may be vestibular (oscillopsia, gait instability) rather than auditory. Avoid concomitant vancomycin, loop diuretics, contrast, NSAIDs and amphotericin where possible, and stop as soon as culture data allow.
