Rescue

Refractory hypoxemia — the rescue ladder

⚙️

When lung-protective ventilation is not enough

Before you escalate, exclude the fixable. A sudden desaturation on the ventilator is not "worsening ARDS" until you have excluded the DOPES list — and you can run it in sixty seconds: Displaced tube (right mainstem or oesophageal — listen, look at the depth, check the EtCO₂), Obstructed tube (mucus plug, kinked, biting — pass a suction catheter), Pneumothorax (especially tension — these patients are on positive pressure with stiff lungs and are at high risk; if in doubt, ultrasound or needle it), Equipment failure (disconnection, ventilator fault — take them off and bag them), Stacked breaths / auto-PEEP (disconnect the circuit and let them exhale fully; if the BP comes back, that was it). People die from a mucus plug while their team is arranging ECMO.

Optimise the basics — then prove you have
Recheck VT, plateau, driving pressure, PEEP, fluid balance.
▾

Confirm: VT genuinely at 6 mL/kg of PBW (re-measure the height); Pplat < 30; ΔP < 15; PEEP from the appropriate table and actually titrated to compliance; fluid balance even-to-negative; the source of sepsis controlled and drained; sedation adequate so the patient is not fighting. Most "refractory" ARDS is under-treated ARDS. Fix these before you reach for anything exotic.

2
PRONE POSITIONING — if P/F < 150
The highest-value intervention in severe ARDS. Do it early, do it long.
▾
ElementThe specifics (from PROSEVA)
IndicationP/F < 150 with FiO₂ ≥ 0.6 and PEEP ≥ 5, after a 12–24 h stabilisation period on lung-protective settings. Prone EARLY — within the first 36–48 h.
Dose≥ 16 consecutive hours per day, repeated daily. (The ATS says > 12 h; PROSEVA did ≥ 16, and PROSEVA is where the mortality benefit came from. Do 16.) A typical rhythm: prone in the late afternoon, supine mid-morning.
Result28-day mortality 16.0% vs 32.8% (HR 0.39); 90-day mortality 23.6% vs 41.0%. NNT ≈ 6. This is one of the largest absolute mortality reductions of any intervention in critical care.
Stop whenP/F ≥ 150 with FiO₂ ≤ 0.6 and PEEP ≤ 10, sustained ≥ 4 h after returning supine.
Absolute contraindicationsUnstable spine or unstable fractures (pelvis, long bone); open abdomen; raised intracranial pressure; severe facial trauma or recent facial surgery.
RelativeRecent sternotomy, massive haemoptysis, a very unstable airway, pregnancy (feasible with bolstering), morbid obesity (feasible — and often the patients who benefit most), high pressor requirement.
Complications — and how to prevent themPressure ulcers (face, chest, iliac crests, knees) — reposition the head q2h, pad everything, and inspect at every turn. Accidental extubation and line dislodgement — the single most feared event; this is why proning takes 5 trained people with one person whose only job is the airway. Also: facial oedema (impressive but benign), brachial plexus injury (swimmer's position, arms not over-abducted), transient desaturation during the turn, vomiting (stop feeds ~1 h before), and corneal abrasion (tape the eyes, lubricate).
__SVG_PRONE__
Prone positioning is not a rescue therapy — it is a treatment. The commonest error is treating it as a last-ditch manoeuvre for the dying patient at 3 a.m. on day 6. PROSEVA proned within 36 hours, and that is where the benefit lives. If your patient has a P/F below 150 after 12–24 h of proper lung-protective ventilation, prone them today — not when they are on three pressors and a PaO₂ of 50. And note: an improvement in oxygenation is not the mechanism of the mortality benefit. Non-responders (by oxygenation) still benefit, because the benefit comes from more homogeneous ventilation and therefore less VILI. So do not stop proning just because the PaO₂ did not improve.
3
Neuromuscular blockade — the ACURASYS vs ROSE problem
Not routine. A targeted, 48-hour rescue.
▾

Two large, well-conducted trials of the same drug at the same dose for the same duration reached opposite conclusions. Understanding why is the whole lesson.

ACURASYS (2010)ROSE (2019)
PopulationP/F < 150, within 48 hP/F < 150, PEEP ≥ 8, within 48 h
InterventionIdentical: cisatracurium 15 mg IV bolus, then 37.5 mg/h infusion × 48 h

ResultImproved 90-day survival (adjusted); ~9% absolute reduction in 28-day mortalityNo difference — 90-day in-hospital mortality 42.5% vs 42.8%
The reconciliation. The trials did not disagree about paralysis — they disagreed about the comparator. ROSE's control patients were lightly sedated and comfortable on high PEEP, and were therefore already not generating injurious effort or dyssynchrony. If the control arm is not hurting itself, abolishing its effort cannot help. So paralysis helps precisely the patient in whom vigorous spontaneous effort is causing harm — severe dyssynchrony, breath-stacking, a high driving pressure you cannot control, or a patient you need flat and still to prone safely. It does not help the calm, synchronous patient. That is why ATS 2024 says "conditional for, in early severe ARDS" and ESICM says "strong against routine use" — and both are right.

Paralysis without adequate sedation is torture, and it is a never-event. A paralysed patient can be fully awake, fully aware, in pain, and unable to move a muscle or signal distress — while every monitor reads "calm." Deep sedation (RASS −4 to −5) must be established BEFORE the paralytic and maintained throughout. Also, while paralysed: monitor train-of-four, provide eye care (they cannot blink — corneal abrasion and ulceration follow fast), give VTE prophylaxis (they cannot move at all), and remember that you have removed the patient's ability to tell you anything — no cough, no grimace, no dyssynchrony to warn you. And they cannot seize visibly.
The 48-hour limit is not arbitrary. Prolonged neuromuscular blockade — especially combined with corticosteroids, which these patients are often also receiving — is a major driver of ICU-acquired weakness, the disability that dominates ARDS survivorship (see the Clinic panel). Stop at 48 h. Then let them wake up.
4
Inhaled pulmonary vasodilators — a bridge, not a treatment
They fix the number. They do not fix the patient.
▾
The trick is elegant. Give a vasodilator as a gas and it can only reach alveoli that are still ventilated. It therefore dilates only the capillaries next to functioning alveoli, diverting pulmonary blood flow away from the shunt and toward the working lung. V̇/Q̇ matching improves and the PaO₂ rises — often dramatically. It also lowers pulmonary vascular resistance, which unloads a failing right ventricle (this is arguably its better indication).

These drugs improve the number on the gas and do not improve survival. That combination is seductive and dangerous: it is very easy to feel you have done something. Use them for exactly two purposes — (1) as a bridge to ECMO or to a therapy that does work, and (2) to unload a failing right ventricle. Do not let a rising PaO₂ on iNO substitute for proning the patient or making the ECMO call.
5
VV-ECMO — and the phone call you should make earlier than you think
Not "oxygenation." Lung rest.
▾
The point of ECMO is not to oxygenate the patient. That is a common misconception, and it leads to it being used too late. The point is that once a machine is doing the gas exchange, you can turn the ventilator down to genuinely non-injurious settings ("lung rest" — e.g. VT 3–4 mL/kg, plateau < 25, rate 10) and stop inflicting VILI while the lung heals. It is the ultimate expression of the idea that runs through this entire guide: the ventilator is the second disease.
ElementSpecifics
ConfigurationVeno-venous (VV) for isolated respiratory failure — drains from and returns to the venous system; it supports the lung, not the heart. (Veno-arterial ECMO is for cardiac failure — a different machine for a different problem.)
Indications — the EOLIA criteriaSevere ARDS, on optimal ventilation, meeting ANY ONE of:
• P/F < 50 mmHg for > 3 hours, OR
• P/F < 80 mmHg for > 6 hours, OR
• pH < 7.25 with PaCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg for > 6 hours (despite RR increased to 35 and vent settings optimised to keep Pplat ≤ 32).
EvidenceEOLIA (Combes, NEJM 2018): 60-day mortality 35% (ECMO) vs 46% (control) — but p = 0.09, so formally negative. Critically, 28% of the control arm crossed over to rescue ECMO (and those who did had ~57% mortality), which drove the result toward the null. A subsequent Bayesian re-analysis found a high posterior probability of benefit. Both ATS and ESICM recommend ECMO for severe ARDS meeting EOLIA criteria — the honest summary is "probably beneficial in the right patient at the right centre," not "proven."
ContraindicationsRelative: mechanical ventilation for > 7 days at high settings (the lung is likely already fibrotic — this is the big one, and it is a time limit, which is why you must call early); age > 65–70; severe immunosuppression; irreversible neurological injury; contraindication to anticoagulation; BMI > 45; and — the real one — no reasonable prospect of recovery or transplant. ECMO is a bridge; you must be able to name what it is a bridge to.
ComplicationsBleeding (the commonest — systemic anticoagulation plus an acquired von Willebrand deficiency from the circuit shearing large vWF multimers), intracranial haemorrhage (the most feared), cannula-site bleeding, thrombosis, haemolysis, infection, and limb ischaemia.
VV-ECMO for isolated respiratory failure. Deoxygenated blood is drained (here from the femoral vein), passed through a membrane oxygenator where it picks up O₂ and offloads CO₂, and returned to the venous system (here the internal jugular). The blood then goes through the heart and lungs as normal — the lung is bypassed for gas exchange but not for circulation, which is precisely why the native heart must still be working for VV to be the right mode.

Make the call EARLY — before you need it. The single strongest relative contraindication to ECMO is having already been ventilated at high settings for more than about 7 days. That means the window closes while you are waiting to see whether the patient turns the corner. Phone the ECMO centre when the patient hits severe ARDS — not when they hit the EOLIA numbers. A conversation costs nothing and can be un-had. A patient who becomes eligible on day 9 is no longer eligible.
🧮

Calculator — Murray Lung Injury Score

The Murray score quantifies the severity of lung injury across four axes rather than the one (oxygenation) that Berlin uses. Its main modern role is as an ECMO referral trigger — it was an entry criterion in the CESAR trial, and a score > 3 is a widely used threshold for referral discussion.

—

Murray JF, Matthay MA, Luce JM, Flick MR. Am Rev Respir Dis 1988;138:720–723. Score = sum of the components ÷ the number of components used (so a component you cannot measure is omitted from both). Interpretation: 0 = no lung injury; 0.1–2.5 = mild-to-moderate; > 2.5 = severe lung injury (ARDS) — and the band at which ECMO referral is conventionally discussed.

Why there is no APACHE / SOFA / LIPS calculator on this page. Those scores use continuous, multi-variable, weighted lookup tables that cannot be faithfully reimplemented from a summary — and a nearly correct severity score is worse than none at all, because it looks authoritative. Where the exact point values are verifiable (P/F, ROX, RSBI, Murray, A–a, PBW) you get a working calculator here. Where they are not, you get a link: MDCalc. The same caution applies to the Oxygenation Index — its published severity bands are validated in children (PALICC); there is no validated adult classification, and quoting the paediatric cut-offs at an adult bedside is a common textbook error.

Pressors, inotropes & the right ventricle

💉

Which drip, why, and the dose

ARDS rarely arrives alone — its commonest cause is sepsis, so most of these patients are also in shock. Target MAP ≥ 65 mmHg (Surviving Sepsis 2021).

The right ventricle is the organ that kills ARDS patients, and almost every ARDS therapy attacks it. Acute cor pulmonale complicates ~20–25% of moderate–severe ARDS. Watch the trap: hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and acidosis all raise pulmonary vascular resistance (hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction) — and so does high PEEP, by compressing alveolar capillaries. So your permissive hypercapnia and your high-PEEP strategy are both loading a ventricle that is already failing. If the RV starts to fail: get an echo; limit driving pressure and plateau; be more cautious with PEEP; relax the permissive hypercapnia (target a lower PaCO₂); prone them (proning genuinely unloads the RV); consider an inhaled pulmonary vasodilator; use norepinephrine and vasopressin in preference to agents that raise PVR; and maintain systemic pressure — the RV is perfused in systole as well as diastole, so systemic hypotension causes RV ischaemia, which begets more RV failure in a spiral.
Comfort

Analgesia, sedation & delirium

😴

Light is better — and the reason is survivorship

Target RASS −2 to 0 (light sedation) in almost everyone. Deep sedation is associated with longer ventilation, more delirium, and — via delirium — with the long-term cognitive impairment that dominates ARDS survivorship (BRAIN-ICU: global cognition at 12 months comparable to mild Alzheimer's in many survivors, with duration of delirium the strongest modifiable predictor). The sedation you give today is the dementia you cause in a year. The exceptions where deep sedation is genuinely required: active neuromuscular blockade, refractory intracranial hypertension, and severe dyssynchrony you cannot otherwise control. Analgesia first ("analgosedation") — much agitation in the ICU is pain.

Liberation from the ventilator

⬇️

Getting the tube out — the algorithm, the numbers, and the "not yet"

Screen every single morning. The commonest cause of prolonged ventilation is not a stubborn lung — it is nobody asking the question. Every extra ventilator day buys VAP, delirium, weakness and death.

The daily liberation algorithm — readiness screen → SAT → SBT → RSBI → cuff leak → extubate → post-extubation support.

Step 1 — the readiness screen (all of these)

DomainThreshold
The cause is improvingThe precipitant is treated or clearly resolving. This is the criterion, not a number.

The forgotten oneElectrolytes repleted — especially phosphate and magnesium. A hypophosphataemic diaphragm cannot do the work, and the patient will fail the SBT for a reason that has nothing to do with their lungs.

Step 2 — SAT, then SBT

SAT (spontaneous awakening trial): stop the sedation. Fail = sustained agitation, RR > 35, SpO₂ < 88%, distress, or arrhythmia. SBT (spontaneous breathing trial): pressure support 5–8 cmH₂O with PEEP 5, or a T-piece, for 30–120 minutes. Coupling SAT to SBT (the "wake up and breathe" protocol) reduces ventilator days and mortality — do them together, in that order, every day.

Step 3 — RSBI, measured during the trial

RSBI = respiratory rate ÷ tidal volume in LITRES. Yang KL, Tobin MJ. N Engl J Med 1991;324:1445–1450. < 105 predicts successful extubation; > 105 predicts failure. Measure it on minimal support (ideally a T-piece or PS 0–5) — measuring the RSBI on high pressure support flatters it and is the commonest way people fool themselves.

The RSBI is simply a number for "rapid and shallow." A failing patient cannot generate a decent tidal volume, so to keep their minute ventilation up they must breathe fast. Rate goes up, volume goes down — and the ratio of the two explodes. It captures, in one number, the thing you can see from the end of the bed.

Step 4 — the cuff-leak test (in at-risk patients only)

Deflate the cuff and measure the difference between inspired and expired tidal volume. A leak < ~110 mL (or < 10–25% of the delivered VT) suggests laryngeal oedema and a risk of post-extubation stridor. Do it in patients at risk: prolonged intubation, a traumatic or repeated intubation, a large tube, or a previous failed extubation. If there is no leak: give methylprednisolone (e.g. 20–40 mg IV, at least 4 hours — ideally 12 — before extubation) and re-test. Do not simply extubate and hope; post-extubation stridor requiring re-intubation in a swollen airway is a genuinely dangerous situation.

Step 5 — "NOT YET, EVEN IF THEY PASSED"

Do not extubate — despite a passed SBT — if:
  • The airway is not protected. Poor cough, copious secretions requiring suctioning more often than ~q2h, or an obtunded patient. A patient can have perfect lungs and still need the tube. Passing an SBT tests the lungs; it says nothing about the airway.
  • No cuff leak in an at-risk patient, and steroids have not been given.
  • They are going straight back to theatre, or need another procedure requiring anaesthesia.
  • Escalating pressors, active ischaemia, or an unstable arrhythmia.
  • A deteriorating neurological status, or an unresolved cause of coma.
  • It is 2 a.m. and the team that can re-intubate has gone home. This is not a physiological criterion, and it is a completely legitimate reason to wait until morning.

Step 6 — after the tube comes out

High-risk patients (age > 65, COPD, heart failure, obesity, hypercapnia, weak cough, > 7 days of ventilation, or more than one comorbidity) should go straight onto prophylactic HFNC or NIV — not conventional oxygen. Applied prophylactically, this reduces re-intubation. Applied as a rescue once the patient is already failing, NIV does not help and may harm by delaying re-intubation. The distinction is everything: prophylaxis works, rescue does not.

Re-intubation within 48 h carries substantially increased mortality. Watch closely — the first 48 hours are the danger window.

Step 7 — when liberation keeps failing

After roughly 7–14 days of failed weaning, discuss tracheostomy (it reduces sedation needs, improves comfort, aids secretion clearance and makes weaning easier — though trials have not shown a mortality benefit for doing it early) — and, in the same conversation, revisit goals of care. The fibrotic-phase ARDS patient who cannot come off the ventilator is heading for an LTAC and a prolonged wean, and the family deserves to understand that trajectory before it happens rather than after.

ICU → floor downgrade criteria

Ready for the floor when ALL of: extubated and stable for ≥ 24 h (or on a stable, established chronic trach/vent plan); SpO₂ ≥ 92% on ≤ 4–6 L/min nasal cannula; RR < 25 with no accessory-muscle use; off all vasopressors ≥ 12–24 h; lactate normalised; mentation at baseline (or a stable, non-escalating delirium); source of infection controlled and on an oral or a stable IV antibiotic plan; and a fluid balance that is even or negative.

NOT YET IF: they were extubated in the last few hours (the re-intubation window is 48 h — a patient extubated at 4 p.m. does not go to the floor at 6 p.m.); the FiO₂ requirement is still climbing; they still need HFNC or NIV and your floor cannot manage it; they are on any pressor; the lactate is still rising; or you have that feeling — which is data — that they will be back in the unit by morning.

Floor

Extubated, stable ≥ 24 h, off pressors, modest O₂ requirement that is falling.

Step-down / IMC

Needs HFNC/NIV or close monitoring but not organ support. New trach with a stable wean plan.

Acute rehab (IRF)

Off the vent, medically stable, and can tolerate ≥ 3 h therapy/day. The right destination for ICU-acquired weakness with the reserve to work.

LTAC

Ventilator-dependent with a tracheostomy, needing a prolonged (> 25 day) wean. The classic fibrotic-phase ARDS destination.

SNF

Off the vent, deconditioned, needs 24-h skilled nursing but < 3 h therapy/day.

Palliative / hospice

Irreversible respiratory failure, or a patient who declines further escalation. Involve palliative care in parallel with active care — from day one, not at the end.
