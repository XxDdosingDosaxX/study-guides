ICU triggers & what the guidelines actually say

🚨

When it belongs in the unit

Any patient meeting the Berlin criteria belongs in an ICU — even Berlin-mild ARDS carries ~27–35% mortality. Beyond that: any FiO₂ > 50% to maintain saturation, failing HFNC (ROX < 3.85 at 12 h) or failing NIV (no improvement at 1–2 h), RR ≥ 35, pH < 7.30 with rising PaCO₂, altered mentation, or any need for vasopressors.

The two governing guidelines — and where they genuinely disagree

You will be told in different hospitals, by different attendings, to do opposite things. That is not because someone is wrong; it is because the two major societies published guidelines within a year of each other and reached different conclusions on two of the biggest questions. Knowing where they diverge — and why — is more useful than memorising either one.

Notice the pattern. The interventions everyone agrees on are the mechanical ones that reduce the injury you are inflicting (low VT, prone, no sustained recruitment). The ones they argue about are the ones that add an intervention on top (high PEEP, paralysis, ECMO). That is a decent heuristic for critical care generally: the strongest evidence is usually for doing less harm, not for doing more things.

The initial settings — and the reasoning behind every one

Tidal volume is dosed on PREDICTED body weight — calculated from HEIGHT and SEX ONLY. Never actual weight. Never estimated weight. Lungs do not grow when a person gains 40 kg. Dosing a 6 mL/kg tidal volume on the actual weight of a 120 kg patient delivers ~720 mL into a lung built for ~400 — you have just prescribed volutrauma with a straight face. Measure the height. Every single time. This is, by a wide margin, the commonest serious ventilator error in general ICUs, and it is fatal at scale.

Why volume control, and not pressure control?

In ARDS the lung's compliance changes hour to hour. In volume control, you set the volume and the pressure varies — so if the lung stiffens, the pressure alarm tells you. In pressure control, you set the pressure and the volume varies — so if the lung stiffens, the tidal volume silently falls, and if it improves, the tidal volume silently rises and you can be delivering an injurious breath without any alarm sounding. Since the thing you are trying to limit IS the volume, set the volume. That is the whole argument, and it is why every major ARDS trial used volume control.

The tidal volume protocol (ARDSNet, verbatim)

  1. Calculate PBW.  Male: 50 + 2.3 × (height in inches − 60).  Female: 45.5 + 2.3 × (height in inches − 60).
  2. Start at VT = 8 mL/kg PBW.
  3. Reduce VT by 1 mL/kg at intervals of ≤ 2 hours until VT = 6 mL/kg PBW.
  4. Check the plateau pressure (0.5 s inspiratory hold) at least q4h and after every change.
    • If Pplat > 30: decrease VT by 1 mL/kg steps — down to a minimum of 4 mL/kg.
    • If Pplat < 25 AND VT < 6: increase VT by 1 mL/kg until Pplat > 25 or VT = 6 (this prevents needless hypoventilation).
    • If Pplat < 30 but the patient is breath-stacking or severely dyssynchronous: VT may be increased to 7 or even 8 mL/kg — dyssynchrony is itself injurious.
  5. Manage the pH (goal 7.30–7.45):
    • pH 7.15–7.30: increase the respiratory rate until pH > 7.30 or PaCO₂ < 25 — maximum set rate 35.
    • pH < 7.15: increase RR to 35; if the pH is still < 7.15, VT may be increased in 1 mL/kg steps (the plateau-pressure limit may be exceeded — this is the one sanctioned exception), and bicarbonate may be considered.
    • pH > 7.45: decrease the respiratory rate.

Driving pressure — the number that matters most

ΔP = Plateau pressure − PEEP = VT / Crs

Amato's mediation analysis of 3,562 patients across nine trials (NEJM 2015) found that driving pressure was the variable most strongly associated with survival — and, crucially, that changes in VT or PEEP only improved survival when they lowered ΔP.

Look at the algebra: ΔP = VT/Crs. Compliance in ARDS is essentially proportional to the size of the aerated baby lung. So driving pressure is tidal volume normalised to the amount of lung you actually have left. Two patients can both be on "6 mL/kg" and be experiencing completely different strain — because one has 1 L of aerated lung and the other has 2.5 L. 6 mL/kg is a population rule. Driving pressure is that rule individualised to the patient in front of you. If ΔP is > 15, you are over-stretching this particular lung, whatever the protocol says. Target ΔP < 15, ideally < 13.

How to actually pick a PEEP, beyond the table. Raise PEEP by 2 and watch three things: (1) the PaO₂/SpO₂ — does oxygenation improve? (2) the compliance / driving pressure — does ΔP fall? and (3) the blood pressure — does the CVP rise and the BP drop? If PEEP improves oxygenation AND lowers driving pressure, you have recruited lung — keep it. If PEEP improves the PaO₂ but the driving pressure rises and the BP falls, you have not recruited anything: you have over-distended the healthy lung and squeezed the heart. Back off. That single test — "what happened to the driving pressure?" — is how you tell recruitment from over-distension at the bedside, and it is worth more than any table.
The static pressure–volume curve — the theory behind PEEP. The lower inflection point (LIP) is where collapsed alveoli begin to snap open; the upper inflection point (HIP) is where the lung starts to over-distend. The "open lung" idea is to keep the tidal breath on the steep, compliant middle of the curve — PEEP above the LIP (so alveoli never fully collapse and shear) and plateau below the HIP (so they never over-stretch). The curve is why both a PEEP that is too low and a pressure that is too high injure the lung.

Permissive hypercapnia — deliberately accepting a bad number

To keep VT at 6 mL/kg in a lung with high dead space, you must accept a rising PaCO₂ and a falling pH. This is not a failure — it is the price of the therapy, and it is a price worth paying. Accept a pH down to ~7.20 (some tolerate 7.15) provided haemodynamics hold. Do not "fix" the CO₂ by raising the tidal volume — that trades a survivable number for an unsurvivable lung. Raise the rate first (to a maximum of 35); beyond that, accept it.
When permissive hypercapnia is dangerous: raised intracranial pressure (CO₂ is a potent cerebral vasodilator — hypercapnia will raise ICP; this is close to an absolute contraindication), severe pulmonary hypertension or RV failure (hypercapnia and acidosis both raise pulmonary vascular resistance and will finish off a struggling RV), severe metabolic acidosis (no respiratory compensation left to give), pregnancy (fetal acidosis), and significant haemodynamic instability (acidaemia reduces catecholamine responsiveness). Also note: at very high rates, the expiratory time shortens and you generate auto-PEEP — which raises the CO₂ further. Check for it (the expiratory flow tracing never returns to zero before the next breath).
