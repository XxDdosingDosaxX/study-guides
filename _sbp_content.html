Pathophysiology & classification

🧬

Mechanism — how gut bacteria end up infecting ascites

SBP is the collision of three failures that only cirrhosis produces together. (1) Portal hypertension engorges and loosens the gut wall and slows transit, feeding small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) and increasing mucosal permeability. (2) Bacterial translocation then lets gut organisms — chiefly E. coli, Klebsiella, and streptococci — cross into mesenteric lymph nodes and the bloodstream, from which they seed the ascitic fluid. (3) The ascites cannot defend itself: cirrhotic ascites is a low-protein, complement-poor, opsonin-poor broth, and cirrhosis blunts reticuloendothelial (Kupffer-cell) clearance — so a few translocated bacteria that would be wiped out in a healthy peritoneum instead multiply unopposed. When the neutrophil response to that infection pushes the ascitic PMN count to ≥250 cells/mm³, that is SBP.

Think of ascites as a warm, still pond with no filter and no lifeguards. A healthy peritoneum is a chlorinated, patrolled pool — a stray bacterium is skimmed out immediately. In cirrhosis the pond's defenses (protein, complement, opsonins) are drained away and the fences around it (gut wall, liver's Kupffer filter) are broken, so the same stray bacterium blooms into an infection.
__SVG_sbppatho_dark__
__SVG_sbppatho_light__
The bacterial-translocation cascade. Each therapy interrupts a specific limb: gut decontamination (norfloxacin/rifaximin) prevents translocation, third-generation cephalosporins kill the seeded Enterobacterales, and albumin defends the arterial circulation to prevent hepatorenal syndrome.
every SBP drug maps to one step above. Cefotaxime/ceftriaxone inhibit cell-wall synthesis in the gut Enterobacterales that seed the fluid. Albumin is oncotic volume plus a scavenger of nitric oxide and endotoxin — it props up effective arterial blood volume so the kidneys don't tip into type-1 hepatorenal syndrome. Norfloxacin / ciprofloxacin / rifaximin decontaminate the gut to stop translocation before it starts. Non-selective β-blockers help variceal pressure but must be used cautiously — in advanced SBP they can drop the already-low arterial pressure and worsen renal perfusion.
🦠

Microbiology — who is in the fluid

Community-acquired SBP is monomicrobial and dominated by gut flora — Gram-negative enteric bacilli cause ~70%, with E. coli the single most common isolate. Growth of multiple organisms, anaerobes, or fungi should instead make you suspect secondary (surgical) peritonitis.

Runyon criteria — is this SBP or a surgical (secondary) peritonitis?

Suspect secondary peritonitis (perforation/abscess) when the neutrocytic fluid meets ≥2 of 3: total protein >1 g/dL, glucose <50 mg/dL, LDH above the upper limit of normal for serum. Additional flags: PMN in the thousands, polymicrobial Gram stain, CEA >5 or ALP >240, and failure of the PMN to fall on the 48-hour repeat tap.

SAAG — proving the ascites is portal-hypertensive

SAAG = serum albumin − ascitic albumin. A gradient ≥1.1 g/dL means portal hypertension (cirrhosis, heart failure, Budd-Chiari) — the substrate on which SBP occurs; <1.1 g/dL points elsewhere (peritoneal carcinomatosis, TB, pancreatic ascites) where "SBP" is not the right frame. Ascitic total protein <1.5 g/dL marks low-opsonin fluid at high risk for a first SBP.

SBP is a great masquerader: the "textbook" peritonitis picture is often muted or absent because cirrhotic ascites separates inflamed peritoneal surfaces and the immune response is blunted. Any unexplained deterioration in a cirrhotic with ascites — encephalopathy, renal decline, hypotension — is SBP until a tap says otherwise.

🩺 Clinic — prevention, prophylaxis & the cirrhotic at risk
Assess

Who is at risk — and when to worry

🔍

The at-risk outpatient

SBP is a disease of advanced cirrhosis with ascites. Roughly 1 in 10 cirrhotics with ascites develops SBP each year, and after a first episode the 1-year recurrence approaches 70% without prophylaxis. The clinic job is to identify the high-risk patient, keep them on the right prophylaxis, and know the moment outpatient management is no longer safe.

Highest-risk phenotypes:

  • Low-protein ascites (ascitic total protein <1.5 g/dL) — poor opsonic defense.
  • Prior SBP — the strongest single predictor of the next episode.
  • Advanced liver failure — Child-Pugh ≥9 with bilirubin ≥3 mg/dL, or renal/circulatory impairment (Cr ≥1.2, BUN ≥25, or Na ≤130).
  • Active or recent GI/variceal bleeding — translocation surges during a bleed.
  • Chronic PPI use — gastric acid suppression promotes SIBO and is an independent, potentially modifiable risk factor.
Tell every ascites patient: new fever, abdominal pain, confusion, or simply "feeling more unwell" = go to the ED for a tap. Waiting for the classic rigid abdomen loses the window.

When clinic isn't enough

🚑

Red flags → ED / admission

Send to the ED for a tap and admission for any of: fever, abdominal pain, or new/worsening confusion in an ascites patient; rising creatinine or reduced urine output; hypotension (SBP <90 / MAP <65); GI bleeding; or simply an unexplained clinical decline. Monitor

What to trend each day

📈

Daily assessment targets

  • Renal function (Cr, BUN, urine output): the single most important trend — a rise means evolving HRS → ensure albumin given, remove nephrotoxins.
  • Mental status: improving encephalopathy signals source control; worsening HE = treatment failure or another precipitant.
  • Hemodynamics (MAP ≥65, HR, lactate clearance): the sepsis trajectory.
  • Repeat paracentesis at 48 h in uncertain cases or non-responders → confirm >25% PMN fall.
  • Antibiotic day count & de-escalation: narrow to culture; plan a 5-day (up to 7) total course.
  • Start secondary prophylaxis (norfloxacin/ciprofloxacin daily) before discharge; refer for transplant evaluation.

Admit

ICU triggers

🚨

When SBP belongs in the unit

SBP earns ICU care when it becomes septic shock or precipitates organ failure — the acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) trajectory. Triggers:

  • Septic shock: MAP <65 or vasopressor requirement after resuscitation; lactate >2 mmol/L persisting.
  • Type-1 HRS-AKI: rapidly rising creatinine despite albumin, especially if oliguric or needing RRT.
  • Grade 3–4 hepatic encephalopathy with airway risk.
  • Respiratory failure (aspiration, ARDS, hepatic hydrothorax) needing NIV or intubation.
  • Concurrent variceal bleeding with instability.

Monitoring & access

🩸

Lines, labs, targets

  • Arterial line for continuous MAP (target ≥65) and frequent lactate/ABG in shock.

  • Dynamic fluid responsiveness (passive-leg-raise, POCUS/IVC, stroke-volume variation) — cirrhotics are vasodilated and easily over-resuscitated; crystalloid excess worsens ascites and tissue edema.

the cirrhotic circulation is already maximally vasodilated (nitric-oxide–driven splanchnic pooling). Norepinephrine restores systemic tone at the α1 receptor; terlipressin works upstream by constricting the splanchnic bed specifically, which is why it reverses HRS while a pure systemic pressor may not. Albumin is the common denominator — it refills the effective arterial volume both are trying to defend.

Airway

Respiratory support

🌬️

When to protect the airway; initial settings

Intubate for grade 3–4 encephalopathy with airway compromise (aspiration risk), refractory hypoxemia, or exhaustion. Tense ascites and hepatic hydrothorax splint the diaphragm — a large-volume paracentesis can improve respiratory mechanics. Use lung-protective settings; keep the head up and consider it a full-stomach/aspiration-risk airway.

Rescue

Advanced / rescue therapies

⚙️

When first-line isn't enough

  • Renal replacement therapy (CRRT) for HRS/AKI with refractory volume, acidosis, or hyperkalemia — as a bridge to recovery or transplant (not a destination in a non-candidate).
  • Terlipressin/norepinephrine + albumin escalation for HRS-AKI (see pressor table); reassess candidacy for TIPS/transplant.
  • TIPS is not a treatment for acute SBP and is contraindicated during active infection/severe HE; consider later for refractory ascites once infection is controlled.
  • Liver transplantation — the only definitive therapy for recurrent SBP / end-stage disease; SBP itself accelerates the transplant timeline.
  • Broaden antimicrobials (carbapenem ± vancomycin/daptomycin/linezolid) guided by local MDR patterns and repeat cultures when failing.
Downgrade

When to downgrade

⬇️

De-escalation to the floor

Downgrade when: off vasopressors ≥12–24 h with MAP ≥65; lactate normalized; renal function stable or improving (no acute RRT need); mentation improving (HE ≤ grade 2, airway secure); respiratory status stable off NIV/extubated; and the antibiotic is narrowed to a culture-guided agent. Not yet if lactate is still rising, creatinine is climbing on albumin, HE remains grade 3–4, or pressors were weaned within the last few hours. For a non-transplant candidate with progressive multi-organ failure, shift the conversation to goals of care rather than repeated escalation.
