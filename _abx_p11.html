Sepsis & the antibiotic clock

🚨

How fast, and how broad

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection — operationally, an acute rise in the SOFA score of ≥2 points attributable to infection. Septic shock is sepsis with vasopressor requirement to keep MAP ≥65 mmHg and a lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate volume resuscitation, and carries hospital mortality above 40%.

Clinical situationAntibiotic timing (Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021)
Septic shock, or sepsis with a high likelihood of infectionImmediately — ideally within 1 hour of recognition
Possible sepsis without shockRapid focused assessment for infectious versus non-infectious cause; if concern persists, antimicrobials within 3 hours of first recognition
Low likelihood of infection, no shockDefer antimicrobials while continuing to monitor closely and search for the cause
In septic shock, mortality rises measurably with every hour of delay to effective antimicrobial therapy. Draw two sets of blood cultures if it can be done in minutes — but never let culture collection delay the first dose in a patient in shock. Give the antibiotic, then keep looking for the source.

The first hour, alongside antibiotics

  • Fluids: at least 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid within 3 hours for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, then reassess with dynamic measures rather than giving fixed further boluses.
  • Lactate: measure at presentation and remeasure within 2–4 h if elevated. A lactate that fails to clear on adequate resuscitation is a source-control problem.
  • Vasopressors: start norepinephrine for MAP ≥65 mmHg — peripherally if needed rather than waiting for central access.
  • Source control: identify and act on a drainable or removable source as soon as medically and logistically practical. This is the intervention with the largest effect size in the entire bundle.

Broad-spectrum & MDRO coverage

🛡️

Which holes to plug, and the evidence for plugging them

Empiric ICU therapy is built by asking the three-holes question — MRSA? Pseudomonas? anaerobes? — and then adding a fourth for the patient with resistance history: ESBL or CRE? Each addition must be justified by a risk factor, because each one carries cost. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign explicitly recommends against empiric MRSA coverage in patients at low risk, and against routine double gram-negative coverage in patients at low risk of multidrug resistance.

Should I double-cover Pseudomonas? Empiric double coverage — two antipseudomonal agents from different classes — is justified only while you are waiting for susceptibilities in a patient with septic shock plus a genuine risk of multidrug resistance, or on a unit where more than about 10% of gram-negative isolates would be resistant to your chosen monotherapy. It is a hedge against picking the wrong empiric drug, not a synergy strategy: once susceptibilities return, drop to a single agent. Continuing double coverage adds nephrotoxicity and resistance without benefit.
The vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam AKI story is a lesson in evidence quality. A large body of observational data suggested this combination roughly tripled AKI risk, and it changed practice across the country. The randomised ACORN trial then compared pip-tazo with cefepime in patients also receiving vancomycin and found no difference in kidney injury — while cefepime looked worse neurologically. The likely explanation for the observational signal is that piperacillin-tazobactam interferes with creatinine secretion, producing a rise in measured creatinine without true injury. Dose vancomycin by AUC, watch the creatinine, and do not choose your gram-negative agent on the basis of the old signal alone.
Dosing

PK/PD in critical illness

⚗️

Why the ward dose is often the wrong ICU dose

Critical illness distorts almost every pharmacokinetic parameter at once. Capillary leak and aggressive fluid resuscitation expand the volume of distribution of hydrophilic drugs — β-lactams, vancomycin, aminoglycosides — so standard doses produce sub-therapeutic concentrations exactly when you can least afford them. Hypoalbuminaemia raises the free fraction of highly protein-bound drugs like ceftriaxone and increases their clearance. And a subgroup of patients develops augmented renal clearance — a CrCl above roughly 130 mL/min, classically in younger patients with sepsis, trauma or burns and no pre-existing renal disease — who clear renally eliminated antibiotics so fast that normal dosing fails.

Cefepime neurotoxicity is an ICU disease. Encephalopathy, myoclonus, and non-convulsive status epilepticus occur when cefepime accumulates — typically in renal impairment with an unadjusted dose, in the elderly, or when a patient's renal function falls mid-course and nobody re-doses. The presentation looks exactly like ICU delirium, so it is routinely treated with antipsychotics instead of being recognised. If a patient on cefepime develops unexplained encephalopathy or myoclonus, stop the drug and get an EEG. Re-check renal dosing on every ICU patient every day.
Source

Source control

🔧

The intervention that outperforms every antibiotic decision

Antibiotics reach bacteria through blood. A walled-off collection has no blood supply, an abscess interior is acidic and hypoxic (which specifically inactivates aminoglycosides), the bacterial inoculum is enormous and largely non-dividing (which specifically defeats β-lactams), and biofilm on hardware is phenotypically tolerant to nearly everything. No antibiotic regimen substitutes for drainage. If a patient is not improving, the question is almost never "which broader antibiotic?" — it is "what have I not drained?"

Neutropenia

Febrile neutropenia

🧬

Fever may be the only sign there is

Definition: absolute neutrophil count <500 cells/µL — or <1,000/µL with an expected decline to below 500 within 48 hours — plus a single oral temperature ≥38.3 °C, or a temperature ≥38.0 °C sustained over one hour. Without neutrophils there is no pus, no infiltrate, and often no localising sign at all: fever alone is the emergency.

Antibiotics within one hour. Do not wait for imaging, a source, or the culture result. Draw blood cultures from a peripheral site and from every lumen of any central line, then give an antipseudomonal β-lactam immediately.

MASCC risk index — is this patient low risk?

Higher scores mean lower risk. Maximum 26 points. Score ≥21 identifies low risk — a candidate for oral and/or outpatient management. The burden-of-illness tiers are mutually exclusive and are not additive.

Downgrade

De-escalation & transfer out

⬇️

Getting off the broad-spectrum regimen and out of the unit

Antibiotic de-escalation in the ICU

  • Reassess every day, and formally at 48–72 hours. De-escalation in the ICU is safe and is not associated with worse outcomes.
  • Narrow to the isolate the moment susceptibilities return — including switching off vancomycin to cefazolin or nafcillin for MSSA.
  • Stop the empiric MRSA agent if the nasal PCR is negative in pneumonia or cultures are negative at 48–72 h in an improving patient.
  • Drop double gram-negative coverage to a single agent once susceptibilities are known.
  • Stop redundant anaerobic coverage — metronidazole alongside pip-tazo, a carbapenem or a cephamycin.
  • Use procalcitonin with clinical criteria, not alone, to support discontinuation: stop when PCT falls below 0.25 ng/mL or has dropped by ≥80% from peak. Note that this only shortens courses if the team actually acts on it.
  • Fix the stop date. Seven days for HAP/VAP; 4 days after source control for intra-abdominal infection; 7 days for uncomplicated gram-negative bacteraemia. Longer courses do not prevent relapse.

A premature downgrade simply bounces the patient back, and bounce-backs do worse than patients who stayed.
ICU disposition beyond the floor. For a patient who cannot be weaned — prolonged ventilation, extensive wounds needing weeks of dressing changes, or multi-week parenteral therapy with ongoing instability — consider LTAC. And for recurrent severe infection in advanced irreversible illness, an honest goals-of-care conversation belongs in the plan: antibiotics can be a comfort measure or a burden, and which one they are depends entirely on what the patient wants.
