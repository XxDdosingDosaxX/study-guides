Symptoms & signs — frequency and the mechanism behind each

Read every finding as evidence for one of two things: forward failure (not enough output → hypoperfusion) or backward failure (blood damming behind the failing chamber → congestion). Forward findings are what make it shock. Frequencies below are approximate, pooled from the SHOCK trial registry and contemporary cardiogenic shock cohorts, and vary by etiology — AMI-CS presents more abruptly than HF-CS.

Finding~FrequencyWhy it happens (pathophysiology)
Hypotension (SBP <90)~90%Stroke volume × heart rate can no longer generate pressure against even a maximally constricted vascular bed. The ~10% who are normotensive are compensating with extreme vasoconstriction — they are not safer, and they crash without warning.
Cool, mottled, clammy extremities~80–90%The single most useful bedside sign. Sympathetic α₁-mediated cutaneous vasoconstriction shunts the last of the cardiac output to brain and heart. Skin is the first organ sacrificed — which is exactly why it warns you first.
Elevated lactate (≥2 mmol/L)~80%Oxygen delivery falls below demand → anaerobic glycolysis → pyruvate shunted to lactate. The biochemical signature of shock, and the number whose trend tells you if your plan is working.
Tachycardia (HR ≥100)~80%Baroreflex attempt to defend CO = SV × HR when SV has collapsed. Self-defeating: it shortens diastole, which is both filling time and coronary perfusion time. Absence of tachycardia is ominous — it means β-blockade, conduction disease, or a heart too sick to mount a response.
Dyspnoea / respiratory distress~75%Rising PCWP transudates fluid into the interstitium and alveoli → stiff, wet lungs + J-receptor stimulation. Also a marker of the work-of-breathing tax: respiratory muscles can consume 20%+ of a cardiac output the patient can't spare.
Oliguria (UOP <30 mL/h)~70%Two hits at once — low forward flow reduces renal perfusion, and high central venous pressure raises renal outflow pressure. Renal perfusion depends on the gradient between them, so congestion damages the kidney as much as low output does.
Elevated JVP~60–70%A direct window onto right atrial pressure. In CS it signals biventricular involvement or RV predominance. A markedly elevated JVP with clear lungs should make you think RV infarct or PE — and stop you from diuresing.
Altered mental status~55–65%Cerebral hypoperfusion once MAP falls below the autoregulatory floor (~60 mmHg, higher in chronic hypertensives). Confusion, agitation or somnolence in a hypotensive cardiac patient is an organ failure, not a personality.
Rales / pulmonary oedema~50–65%Alveolar flooding once PCWP exceeds roughly 25 mmHg. Notably absent in RV-predominant shock and often quiet in chronic HF, where lymphatic adaptation clears fluid faster.
Narrow pulse pressure (<25% of SBP)~50%Pulse pressure tracks stroke volume. A narrow one means the ventricle is ejecting very little per beat — an underused, free bedside surrogate for low cardiac output.
Chest pain~40–50%Present when the cause is ischaemic — but absent in roughly half, particularly in diabetics, the elderly, women, and all of HF-CS. Its absence never rules out an infarct as the trigger.
S3 gallop~30–40%Rapid early-diastolic filling striking a stiff, volume-loaded ventricle. Insensitive but highly specific for elevated filling pressure — if you hear it, believe it.
New murmur~5–10%The finding that changes everything. A new holosystolic murmur days 2–7 post-MI = papillary muscle rupture (acute MR) or ventricular septal rupture until proven otherwise. Get an echo now and call surgery. Beware: with torrential acute MR the murmur may be soft or absent because atrial and ventricular pressures equalise.
Pulsus paradoxus / muffled sounds / distended neck veins<5%Beck's triad — tamponade. Rare but reversible in minutes with a needle. This is why every shock patient gets an echo before anything invasive.
Read the patternThe exam finding with the best signal-to-noise is cool extremities plus a rising lactate. You can be fooled by a blood pressure (compensated, or artefactually cuffed), by rales (absent in RV shock), and by chest pain (absent in half). You are rarely fooled by a cold, mottled patient whose lactate is climbing.
Four presentations that must trigger an immediate echo before you do anything else, because each has a specific fix that no drug replicates: (1) new murmur + shock post-MI → papillary muscle or septal rupture → surgery; (2) Beck's triad → tamponade → pericardiocentesis; (3) high JVP + clear lungs + inferior STEMI → RV infarct → fluids, not diuresis; (4) high JVP + clear lungs + hypoxia + RV strain → massive PE → thrombolysis.

Imaging & ECG — what to look for and when it appears

Per the 2025 ACC guidance, once CS is suspected, a 12-lead ECG, chest radiograph, and transthoracic echo (or POCUS) should be completed as soon as possible. The echo is the highest-yield test in medicine here: it separates the four "fix-it-now" diagnoses above from plain pump failure in about ninety seconds.

Study / findingWhen it appears / thresholdWhy — and what it changes
ECG — ST elevationImmediately (<10 min of arrival)Transmural injury current. Activates the cath lab. Anterior/left main territory carries the highest CS risk. Also look for new LBBB and for ST depression in V1–V3 (which may be a posterior STEMI — get posterior leads).
ECG — right-sided leads (V4R)If inferior STEMIST elevation in V4R = RV infarction. Do this reflexively on every inferior MI — it inverts your management (fluids, avoid nitrates/morphine, preserve AV synchrony).
Echo — LVEF and regional wall motionWithin the first hourConfirms the pump as the culprit and localises the territory. Note: EF correlates poorly with shock severity — plenty of CS patients have an EF of 30% and plenty of stable outpatients do too. The shock is about output and perfusion, not the ejection fraction.
Echo — pericardial effusionFirst 90 secondsRA/RV diastolic collapse + IVC plethora + respirophasic mitral inflow variation = tamponade. Reversible immediately. Never miss it.
Echo — RV size & functionFirst hourDilated, hypokinetic RV with septal flattening = RV-predominant shock or massive PE. TAPSE <17 mm suggests RV dysfunction. This determines whether you support the right heart or the left.
Echo — new MR / VSDWhenever a murmur or sudden deterioration appearsColour Doppler shows the flail leaflet or the trans-septal jet. Surgical emergency. These are the patients who die because everyone assumed it was pump failure.
Echo — VTI / stroke volumeFirst hour, and seriallyLVOT VTI is a non-invasive surrogate for stroke volume — a VTI <15 cm suggests a low output state. Serial VTI is a cheap way to watch response to inotropes when you have no PA catheter.
CXR — cephalizationEarliest (PCWP ~13–18)Rising pulmonary venous pressure recruits upper-lobe vessels normally collapsed when upright.
CXR — Kerley B linesPCWP ~18–25Fluid distending the interlobular septa — interstitial oedema. Precedes the alveolar stage.
CXR — alveolar "bat-wing" oedemaPCWP >25Interstitial capacity exceeded; alveoli flood. Now the patient is hypoxaemic and feeding Loop 2.
CXR — normal-sized heartIn acute CSAn acute insult has had no time to remodel the chamber. A big heart suggests chronic HF-CS; a normal heart with florid oedema suggests an acute mechanical or ischaemic catastrophe.
POCUS — B-lines & IVCAt the bedside, minute oneDiffuse bilateral B-lines = interstitial oedema (correlates with PCWP). A plethoric, non-collapsing IVC supports high right-sided pressures and argues hard against fluid.
Coronary angiographyEmergently in AMI-CS — do not waitBoth diagnostic and therapeutic. In CS, angiography is indicated regardless of time from symptom onset and regardless of whether it's STEMI or NSTEMI — the usual time windows do not apply.
Radiographic congestion lags the haemodynamics by hours, and a substantial minority of acute presentations have a near-normal film. A clear chest X-ray never excludes cardiogenic shock — and in RV-predominant shock the lungs are clear by definition. Correlate with the exam, POCUS and the lactate; never let a reassuring film slow you down.
Anterior STEMI — the highest-risk substrate for cardiogenic shock. ST elevation across the anterior precordial leads means a large territory of LV is dying. In a hypotensive patient, this ECG is the cath-lab activation. Colour-coded tracing showing ST elevation, tachycardia and anterior fascicular block.

The culprit, on angiography. A tight stenosis in a proximal vessel. In AMI-CS this is both the diagnosis and the treatment — but per CULPRIT-SHOCK you open this vessel only and leave the bystander lesions for a staged procedure.

Kerley B lines — the interstitial stage. Short horizontal septal lines reaching the pleura, appearing around a PCWP of 18–25 mmHg. This is Loop 2 becoming visible: the lungs are drowning because the ventricle can't empty.
__CRED_pulm_edema__
The apical four-chamber view — the highest-yield 90 seconds in the resuscitation. In one sweep you get LV function, RV size, valve integrity and the pericardium — which is to say, you separate plain pump failure from tamponade, RV infarct, and acute valve rupture, each of which has a completely different fix.
__CRED_echo__
Pericardial effusion → tamponade. Obstructive, not cardiogenic — but it presents identically as shock with a high JVP, and it is reversed in minutes by a needle. Post-MI, a new effusion with shock means free-wall rupture until proven otherwise. This is why the echo comes before the pressors.

Reading the labs — what each value is telling you, and what fools it

The 2025 ACC guidance packages the initial labs under the SUSPECT CS mnemonic — CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, troponin, natriuretic peptides, lactate, and a blood gas — alongside the clinical assessment for congestion and hypoperfusion. Here's what each one is actually telling you, and how each can lie to you.

LabWhat it means & why it movesWhat raises/lowers it falsely
LactateThe master variable. Rises when O₂ delivery falls below demand → anaerobic glycolysis. ≥2 mmol/L = at least SCAI Stage C; ≥8 mmol/L is a Stage E criterion. Its trend over 2–6 h is the single best real-time read on whether your resuscitation is working — far better than any single blood pressure.Falsely high: epinephrine infusions (β₂-driven aerobic glycolysis — this is the confound that muddied OptimaCC), seizure, metformin, liver failure (impaired clearance), bowel/limb ischaemia, mesenteric ischaemia, malignancy, alcohol. Falsely reassuring: a chronic HF patient may sit at a normal lactate with a genuinely dire cardiac index.
TroponinMyocyte necrosis. A steeply rising/large-magnitude level points to AMI-CS and sends you to the cath lab. But a modest elevation is near-universal in any shock — subendocardial supply-demand mismatch, RV strain, and direct cytokine-mediated injury all leak troponin.Falsely attributed: CKD (reduced clearance → chronically elevated baseline; look at the delta, not the absolute), myocarditis, PE, sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, cardiac contusion, post-arrest. The mistake is assuming any troponin rise in shock means occlusive MI.
Mixed / central venous O₂ sat (SvO₂ / ScvO₂)The most physiologically elegant number in the ICU. As output falls, tissues extract more O₂ per pass → venous saturation falls. SvO₂ <65% (from the PA catheter) means the output is not meeting demand; normal ≥65% per SCAI Stage A criteria. Rising SvO₂ with a falling lactate is the signature of a working plan.Falsely high: sepsis/mixed shock (impaired extraction), left-to-right shunt from a VSD (an "oxygen step-up" — a normal-looking SvO₂ in a crashing post-MI patient should make you hunt for a septal rupture), cyanide toxicity, hypothermia. Falsely low: anaemia, hypoxaemia, shivering, fever, agitation.
Creatinine / BUNCardiorenal syndrome. Renal perfusion depends on the gradient between arterial inflow and venous outflow — so congestion (high CVP) injures the kidney as much as low forward flow does. A high BUN:Cr ratio signals prerenal/RAAS activation. Renal function is embedded in both the IABP-SHOCK II score and SCAI Stage C.Lags injury by 24–48 h — a normal creatinine early does not mean healthy kidneys. Falsely low in cachexia/low muscle mass (an elderly HF patient's "normal" 0.9 may represent a GFR of 35). Contrast from angiography confounds interpretation afterwards.
LFTs / bilirubinTwo distinct patterns, and they mean different things. Cholestatic (↑alk phos, ↑bilirubin, mild AST/ALT) = chronic congestive hepatopathy from high right-sided pressures — a backward sign. A massive AST/ALT spike into the thousands = ischaemic hepatitis ("shock liver") from acute hypoperfusion — a forward sign, and a marker of severity (a SCAI Stage C/D criterion).Confounded by pre-existing liver disease, alcohol, paracetamol, statins, passive congestion from any cause. The AST:ALT ratio and the tempo distinguish shock liver (explosive, peaks in 24–72 h, resolves fast with perfusion) from most other hepatitides.
NT-proBNP / BNPReleased when ventricular myocytes are stretched — so it tracks filling pressure/wall tension, not output. Useful to confirm the heart as the culprit and to rule HF out when low. A rising BNP is a SCAI Stage D marker.Falsely high: age, renal dysfunction, AF, RV strain/PE, sepsis. Falsely low: obesity. Note the trap — sacubitril/valsartan (ARNI) raises BNP but not NT-proBNP because BNP is a neprilysin substrate; follow NT-proBNP in anyone on an ARNI.
Arterial blood gas — pH & base deficitSeverity marker. pH <7.2 or base deficit >10 mEq/L are explicit SCAI Stage E criteria. Severe acidosis is not just a number: it blunts catecholamine responsiveness, so your pressors stop working just when you need them most — a mechanistic reason the spiral accelerates.Mixed pictures are the norm: respiratory compensation, concurrent respiratory acidosis from fatigue, iatrogenic hyperchloraemia from saline, citrate from CRRT.
SodiumHyponatraemia = non-osmotic ADH release driven by low effective arterial blood volume. It's a marker of neurohormonal severity and worse prognosis, not simply "too much water."Thiazides, SIADH, adrenal insufficiency, pseudohyponatraemia from hyperlipidaemia/hyperproteinaemia.
GlucoseStress hyperglycaemia from the catecholamine surge. Independently prognostic — >191 mg/dL (10.6 mmol/L) scores a point in IABP-SHOCK II.Known diabetes, steroids, dextrose-containing infusions, catecholamine drips themselves.
CBCAnaemia worsens O₂ delivery at any given cardiac output (DO₂ = CO × Hb × SaO₂ × 1.34) — a modifiable lever most people forget. Leucocytosis supports the Loop-4 inflammatory arm. Platelets matter enormously if MCS is coming — Impella and ECMO both cause haemolysis and consumptive thrombocytopenia.Haemoconcentration from diuresis, stress demargination of leucocytes, dilution from resuscitation fluids.
If you can only follow two numbers on a cardiogenic shock patient, follow lactate and urine output. Both are direct read-outs of whether organs are being perfused, both respond within hours, and neither can be faked by a pressor the way a blood pressure can. A patient whose MAP you've "fixed" to 70 with escalating norepinephrine while their lactate climbs is getting worse, not better — you have treated the monitor instead of the patient.
