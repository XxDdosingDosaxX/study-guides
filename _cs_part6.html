The things that actually change what you do

1 · The echo comes before the pressors. Four diagnoses masquerade as pump failure and each has a fix that no vasopressor substitutes for: tamponade (needle), massive PE (lysis), RV infarct (fluids — the opposite of what you were about to do), and acute valve/septal rupture (surgeon). Ninety seconds of ultrasound sorts them.
2 · Cardiogenic shock is now more often a heart failure disease than an MI disease. HF-CS has overtaken AMI-CS in contemporary registries — but every landmark trial (SHOCK, IABP-SHOCK II, CULPRIT-SHOCK, DanGer Shock, ECLS-SHOCK) was run in AMI-CS. Be conscious every time you extrapolate that evidence to an end-stage HFrEF patient, because you're doing it without data.
3 · Normotensive shock is real. SCAI Stage C explicitly does not require hypotension, and patients with hypoperfusion without hypotension die more often than those with hypotension and preserved perfusion. Cold hands and a rising lactate beat any cuff reading. Chronic HF patients are the trap — they've adapted to low output and look falsely reassuring.
4 · Norepinephrine, not dopamine — and never phenylephrine. SOAP II: dopamine roughly doubled arrhythmias (24.1% vs 12.4%) with a mortality signal in the CS subgroup. Phenylephrine is pure afterload with no inotropy and can drop cardiac output outright — its only real indication is LVOT obstruction, where you actually want the afterload.
5 · Titrate each drug to its own target. Norepinephrine to MAP ≥65. Dobutamine to lactate, urine output and cardiac index — never to blood pressure, because it's a β₂ vasodilator and chasing pressure with it makes the pressure worse.
6 · Perfusion before decongestion. The commonest way a resident converts a Stage B/C patient into a Stage D one is diuresing a cold patient. If the creatinine climbs and the pressure drops as you diurese, they aren't diuretic-resistant — they're hypoperfused. Re-profile before you double the Lasix.
7 · Open the culprit only. CULPRIT-SHOCK showed immediate multivessel PCI increased death and renal failure (43.3% vs 51.6% death, p=0.03 favouring culprit-only). More contrast, longer procedure, more no-reflow, in a patient with no reserve. Restraint is the intervention.
8 · SHOCK "failed" its primary endpoint and still changed the world. 30-day mortality 46.7% vs 56.0% was not significant (p=0.11); the curves separated at 6 months (50.3% vs 63.1%, p=0.027) and stayed separated for years. Revascularised myocardium needs time. Revascularise early even though the benefit shows up late — and read primary endpoints with this trial in mind.
9 · The mortality cliff is between SCAI C and D (12.4% → 40.4%). That's why SCAI tells spoke centres to transfer on progression to Stage D — before Stage E. The trigger is mechanical and easy to remember: reaching for a second vasoactive agent means the patient should already be moving.
10 · Every catecholamine is also a poison. They all raise myocardial O₂ consumption and arrhythmogenicity in an ischaemic, irritable heart — feeding Loops 1 and 3 even as they help. No pressor or inotrope has a proven mortality benefit in CS. That is the entire case for mechanical support: flow without the metabolic tax.
11 · MCS is not one thing, and the trials aren't interchangeable. IABP (+0.5 L/min) was neutral. VA-ECMO gives huge flow but raises LV afterload and was neutral with more bleeding and limb ischaemia. The microaxial pump — the only device that adds flow and unloads the LV simultaneously — is the only one with a positive mortality trial (DanGer Shock, NNT ≈ 8), in STEMI-CS specifically. The mechanism predicts the result.
12 · Milrinone vs dobutamine is a physiology choice, not an outcome one. DOREMI found no difference (49% vs 54%, p=0.47). So pick on mechanism: milrinone if β-blocked (works downstream of the receptor) or pulmonary hypertension/RV failure (lowers PVR); dobutamine if renal failure (milrinone is ~90% renally cleared and accumulates) or if the BP is marginal (2-minute half-life — if it hurts, it's gone).
13 · Right ventricular shock inverts everything. High JVP + clear lungs + inferior STEMI = get V4R. If it's an RV infarct: give fluid, avoid nitrates and morphine, avoid diuretics, preserve AV synchrony, and check PAPi (<0.9 → consider RV support). An LV-only device in RV failure can make it worse. Diuresing an RV infarct is a classic lethal error.
14 · Intubation can arrest a shock patient. Sedation ablates the sympathetic tone holding the pressure up, positive pressure cuts venous return, and apnoeic acidosis blunts your catecholamines. Have the pressor up before you push drugs, halve the sedative, use etomidate, and let your best operator go first.
15 · Days 2–7 post-MI, a new murmur plus sudden collapse is a mechanical complication until an echo says otherwise. Papillary muscle rupture or VSD — a surgical emergency, not "worsening heart failure." And with torrential acute MR the murmur can be soft or absent, because atrial and ventricular pressures equalise. Don't be reassured by a quiet chest.
16 · Decide the destination before the device. Recovery, durable LVAD, transplant, or palliation? A device with no destination is a slower death with more tubes. SCAI says it plainly: transfers for futile care don't change outcomes and deny capacity to patients who might benefit.
17 · The best cardiogenic shock treatment is the GDMT you started two years ago — and the advanced-HF referral you made before the crash. By the time someone is Stage D on two pressors, LVAD/transplant candidacy is being decided by strangers reading a chart at 3am. I-NEED-HELP exists so that conversation happens in clinic instead.
18 · Don't chase the MAP with the norepinephrine dial while the lactate climbs. That combination means you're buying pressure with afterload the ventricle can't pay for. CPO (MAP × CO / 451, target >0.6 W) can't be fooled that way, because it multiplies pressure by flow. A MAP of 70 with a CPO of 0.45 W is a patient in far more trouble than the chart suggests.

Sources, trials & media

Guidelines & consensus statements

  • 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance: Evaluation and Management of Cardiogenic Shock (Sinha et al.) — the current US anchor document; SUSPECT CS mnemonic, 1-hour/24-hour roadmaps, shock teams & the "shock champion", tMCS selection. JACC · ACC summary
  • SCAI SHOCK Stage Classification Expert Consensus Update (2022) (Naidu et al.) — stages A–E criteria (Table 3), the revised cardiac-arrest modifier, the 3-axis model. Endorsed by ACC, ACEP, AHA, ESC-ACVC, ISHLT, SCCM, STS. JSCAI · JACC · Bedside checklist (PDF)
  • Contemporary Management of Cardiogenic Shock — AHA Scientific Statement (van Diepen et al., Circulation 2017) — pathophysiology, regionalised systems of care. Circulation
  • 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Heart Failure Guideline — GDMT, advanced HF, inotropes, milrinone in renal failure. Circulation
  • 2023 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Acute Coronary Syndromes — CS management; advises against routine IABP in patients without mechanical complications. Eur Heart J
  • 2021 ESC Heart Failure Guidelines — acute HF and CS profiles. Eur Heart J
  • USPSTF: Hypertension (A) · Tobacco (A) · Statins (B) · Prediabetes/T2DM (B) · AAA (B)
