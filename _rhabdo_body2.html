🩺 Clinic — outpatient recognition, statin myopathy & risk stratification
Assess

When to suspect it in clinic

🔍

The outpatient faces of rhabdomyolysis

In clinic, rhabdomyolysis almost never arrives announcing itself. It arrives as four recognisable stories, and your job is to notice that a CK is worth sending.

  1. "I overdid it at the gym." A previously sedentary person 24–72 h after an unaccustomed, eccentric-heavy workout (spin class, CrossFit, a bootcamp, a first marathon), with severe muscle soreness out of proportion to the effort, stiffness, and dark urine. Risk multiplies with heat, humidity, dehydration, and sickle cell trait. Not "just DOMS" if the pain is severe, the muscles are swollen, or the urine is dark.
  2. "My legs ache since the new pill." A statin patient — especially one who recently had a dose increase, or a new interacting drug (clarithromycin, an azole, diltiazem, gemfibrozil) added. Symmetric, proximal, aching muscles. See the dedicated section below.
  3. "I was on the floor all night." An older adult after a fall or a long lie; a patient after alcohol or opioid intoxication; a post-ictal patient. Pressure necrosis over a bony prominence is the tell. These patients are frequently asymptomatic from a muscle standpoint and are found only because someone checked a chemistry panel.
  4. "The CK on the panel is 3,400 and I feel fine." Increasingly the commonest presentation. An incidental CK, often sent for something else. Do not dismiss it — but also do not reflexively admit it. Risk-stratify.
Ask the two questions that change everything: "What is the colour of your urine, and how much are you making?" A patient with dark urine, or one who says they have barely passed urine since the symptoms began, is not an outpatient — they are an ED patient, today.

What to order & when

Treat

Outpatient management — first-line → escalation

💊

Who can you actually treat at home?

There is no drug for rhabdomyolysis. Outpatient management is exactly two things: remove the trigger (which stops muscle dying) and keep the kidney perfused and the urine flowing (which stops the myoglobin from precipitating). Everything below is a variation on those two ideas.
1
Low-risk: remove the trigger + oral hydration
CK <5,000, McMahon <6, normal creatinine & K⁺, making urine, reliable, asymptomatic
▾

Who qualifies — all of the following:

  • CK <5,000 U/L (and ideally falling or clearly explained)
  • McMahon score <6 (see the calculator below)
  • Normal creatinine and normal potassium
  • Normal bicarbonate, calcium and phosphate
  • Making urine normally; urine not dark
  • A benign, removable trigger — exercise, a statin, a brief seizure — not crush, not a long lie, not sepsis
  • Able to drink freely, and reliable for a 24–72 h recheck

What to do:

  • Stop the offending agent — hold the statin, stop the supplement, treat the hypothyroidism, stop the exercise.
  • Oral fluids, aggressively: aim for a urine output the patient can see is generous and pale. There is no trial-derived oral volume; a practical target is ~3 L/day of water/electrolyte fluid in an adult with normal cardiac and renal function, adjusted down for heart failure or CKD.
  • Complete rest from exercise until CK has fallen and symptoms have resolved.
  • Avoid all nephrotoxins — NSAIDs (see the drugs-to-avoid box), contrast, ACE-I/ARB temporarily if volume-depleted.
  • Safety-net explicitly: "Come to the ED if your urine turns dark or cola-coloured, if you are passing much less urine, if the muscle pain worsens or a limb becomes tight and swollen, or if you feel faint or your heart races."
Recheck CK and a BMP in 24–72 h. The CK must be falling. A CK that is flat or rising means muscle is still dying — that patient needs the ED, not another week of water.
2
Intermediate: IV fluids + observation
CK 5,000–15,000, or any risk feature, but well and no organ dysfunction
▾

These patients need intravenous fluid — an infusion centre, an ED, or an observation unit, not a water bottle and a follow-up appointment. Trigger for this rung: CK 5,000–15,000, or McMahon ≥6, or any of: mild AKI, dark urine, an ongoing trigger, significant comorbidity (CKD, heart failure, diabetes), poor oral intake, or a patient you cannot rely on to return.

  • Isotonic crystalloid — see the Wards fluid table for the full agent-by-agent comparison and rates.
  • Reassess after several hours of fluid with a repeat BMP and CK, and a documented urine output.
  • Falling CK, good urine output, normal renal function and potassium → may complete recovery at home with close follow-up.
  • Anything else → admit.
3
Admit — do not manage this in clinic
Any red flag below
▾

See "When clinic isn't enough" below for the full numeric list. In short: any AKI, any hyperkalemia, any hypocalcemia with symptoms, CK >15,000–20,000, dark urine with oliguria, a tense limb, crush or a long lie, or an ongoing trigger you cannot switch off.

Statin-associated muscle symptoms — the clinic's real drug problem

💊

SAMS: define it, grade it, then rebuild the regimen

Statins are the most common drug cause of rhabdomyolysis you will meet in clinic — but true statin rhabdomyolysis is rare (on the order of 1–3 per 100,000 patient-years), while muscle complaints on a statin are extremely common (5–10%+ in practice, though far lower in blinded trials — the nocebo effect is real and large). The clinical skill is distinguishing the three, because the management diverges completely.

EntityCKPictureWhat to do
Myalgia (SAMS)Normal or <4× ULNSymmetric, proximal aching/cramping in large muscle groups. No weakness, no pigmenturia.Statin can often be continued or modified. Work through the rebuild ladder below.
Myositis / myopathy>10× ULN (i.e. >~2,000 U/L)Muscle symptoms with a clearly elevated CK.Stop the statin. Look for an interacting drug, hypothyroidism, vitamin D deficiency. Recheck CK until it normalises.
Rhabdomyolysis>40× ULN (often ≫10,000)Severe symptoms + myoglobinuria and/or renal injury.Stop the statin. Send to the ED. This is the disease this whole guide is about.

Categories per the European Atherosclerosis Society consensus and the National Lipid Association SAMS statement (2022). "ULN" for CK ≈200 U/L, so 10× ULN ≈2,000 and 40× ULN ≈8,000.

Why statins hurt muscle at all. HMG-CoA reductase does not only make cholesterol — it is the head of the mevalonate pathway, which also produces coenzyme Q10 (mitochondrial electron transport) and the isoprenoids that prenylate small GTPases. Blocking it depletes myocyte CoQ10 and destabilises membrane signalling, impairing ATP generation in a cell type with an enormous ATP demand. Go back to the cascade: less ATP → the calcium pumps fail → calcium floods in. A statin is simply another way of cutting the power to the dam. That also explains why risk is dose-dependent and why anything that raises the statin's blood level (a CYP3A4 inhibitor, gemfibrozil, renal or hepatic impairment) multiplies it.

The interactions that actually cause statin rhabdomyolysis. Almost every case of severe statin myopathy has a second drug in it. Memorise these.
  • Gemfibrozil + any statin — avoid outright. Gemfibrozil blocks statin glucuronidation and OATP1B1 uptake, sending statin levels through the roof. If a fibrate is genuinely needed, use fenofibrate, which does not do this.
  • Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors + simvastatin/lovastatin/atorvastatin: clarithromycin, erythromycin, itraconazole, ketoconazole, voriconazole, HIV/HCV protease inhibitors, cyclosporine, and grapefruit juice. Hold the statin during a short course of clarithromycin — a genuinely common real-world save.
  • Simvastatin dose caps (FDA): with amlodipine or amiodarone → max 20 mg; with verapamil or diltiazem → max 10 mg. These caps exist precisely because rhabdomyolysis happened.
  • Colchicine + statin: both are independently myotoxic; the combination compounds it, especially in CKD.
  • Daptomycin + statin: both raise CK. Most clinicians hold the statin during a daptomycin course and monitor CK weekly.

The SAMS rebuild ladder — how to get a statin back on board

About 70% of patients who report intolerance to one statin will tolerate another statin or another regimen. Do not abandon the statin after one bad experience — this is a mortality drug.

1
Hold, confirm causality, and fix the reversible cause
Dechallenge — and go looking for the second hit
▾
  • Hold the statin for 2–4 weeks. If symptoms do not resolve off the drug, the statin was probably never the culprit — and if they worsen, think anti-HMGCR myopathy.
  • Correct the amplifiers: check and treat hypothyroidism (a major and correctable multiplier), replete vitamin D, and — most importantly — remove the interacting drug (see the red box above).
  • Reassess renal and hepatic function; both raise statin exposure.
2
Rechallenge — lower dose, different statin, or intermittent dosing
Three levers, in this order
▾
  • Same statin, lower dose. SAMS is dose-dependent, so this alone often works — e.g. atorvastatin 40 mg → 10 mg.
  • Different statin, preferring a hydrophilic, non-CYP3A4 agent: pravastatin 20–40 mg or rosuvastatin 5 mg (or fluvastatin XL 80 mg).
  • Intermittent dosing, exploiting the long half-lives: rosuvastatin 5–10 mg two or three times weekly, or atorvastatin every other day. Gives most of the LDL benefit at a fraction of the exposure. (Reasonable, widely-used practice; supported by small studies rather than outcome trials.)
3
Truly intolerant — non-statin LDL lowering
Full doses; none of these carry meaningful myopathy risk

Context

How comorbidities change this disease

🔗

The company rhabdomyolysis keeps

ComorbidityHow it changes the diseaseHow it changes your management
Chronic kidney disease Far less renal reserve — a given myoglobin load causes much more AKI. Potassium and phosphate excretion are already impaired, so hyperkalemia arrives earlier and harder. Reduced statin clearance raises myopathy risk. The central tension of this disease: they need aggressive fluid, and they tolerate it worst. Give isotonic fluid but with closer, more frequent reassessment (exam, weights, strict I/O) rather than a fixed high rate. Lower the threshold to involve nephrology early. Cap rosuvastatin at 10 mg if CrCl <30.
Heart failure The disease demands litres; the ventricle cannot take them. Volume overload and pulmonary oedema are a genuine iatrogenic risk of correct rhabdomyolysis treatment. Do not give a blind 1–2 L/hr. Give smaller boluses with reassessment after each; consider more invasive/dynamic assessment of fluid responsiveness. This is a patient in whom you accept a slightly lower urine-output target rather than drown them. Involve critical care early if you cannot achieve urine output without overload.
Diabetes mellitus DKA and HHS both cause rhabdomyolysis (hyperosmolarity, phosphate depletion). Diabetic patients also have higher baseline CKD and more diabetic myonecrosis. In DKA/HHS with an unexplained CK rise, treat both. Watch phosphate closely — it starts low in DKA and then rises as muscle lyses.
Hypothyroidism Causes a myopathy with raised CK in its own right, and substantially increases statin myopathy risk. Check a TSH in every unexplained CK elevation and every statin-intolerant patient. Treating the thyroid often lets a previously "statin-intolerant" patient tolerate the statin. Cheap, correctable, frequently missed.
Alcohol use disorder A triple hit: direct myotoxicity, immobilisation/long lie during intoxication, plus hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia — which themselves cause rhabdomyolysis. Seizures from withdrawal add exertional injury. Look for and correct K⁺ and PO₄³⁻ — but remember they will flip high as muscle lyses, so re-check rather than replacing on autopilot. Treat withdrawal aggressively (benzodiazepines) to prevent seizures. Give thiamine.
Sickle cell trait A well-documented, substantial risk multiplier for exertional rhabdomyolysis (military and collegiate athlete data), via sickling in the hypoxic, acidotic, hyperthermic exercising muscle. Counsel explicitly on graded acclimatisation, hydration, heat, and rest. Do not let a trait-positive athlete do unaccustomed maximal exertion in the heat.
Polypharmacy / older age Age is an independent McMahon variable. More interacting drugs, more CKD, more falls and long lies, less physiological reserve. Reconcile the medication list personally. The most common preventable cause of statin rhabdomyolysis is a new interacting drug added by a different prescriber.
Malignant hyperthermia susceptibility (RYR1) Latent until triggered — then catastrophic. Also causes recurrent exertional rhabdomyolysis without any anaesthetic. Flag it in the chart in capital letters. Volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine must be avoided; dantrolene must be available. This single piece of documentation can prevent a death.

How to act on it. Score <6 → low risk (roughly <3% risk of death or AKI requiring RRT): these patients can often be managed with less intensive fluid and, if everything else is reassuring, sometimes as outpatients. Prevent

Prevention, return-to-play & USPSTF A/B

🏃

Exertional rhabdomyolysis — the staged return to activity

From the Clinical Practice Guidelines for Exertional Rhabdomyolysis (American College of Sports Medicine, Current Sports Medicine Reports 2021 — a military-medicine perspective). The principle: do not let them train again until the muscle has actually healed, because a second insult on top of an unhealed first is how a healthy 20-year-old ends up on dialysis.

1
Phase 1 — rest (minimum 72 hours)
No training at all
▾

Rest or light indoor activity only. 7–8 hours of sleep per night, generous fluid intake, and increased dietary sodium. Recheck CK and urinalysis every 72 h. Do not advance until CK <5× ULN, urinalysis normal, and no symptoms (no pain, soreness, swelling or weakness). If not met, stay in Phase 1 and repeat in another 72 h.

2
Phase 2 — light activity
Begin only once Phase 1 criteria are met
▾

Light, self-paced activity. If symptoms return, drop back. If the patient stays symptom-free, advance.

3
Phase 3 — graded return to regular training
Progressive, supervised
▾

Gradual return to normal sport and physical training, escalating intensity stepwise while remaining symptom-free.

4
Phase 4 — full participation
Unrestricted
▾

Full return. High-risk features that should slow all of this down and prompt specialist input: CK >100,000, AKI, a personal or family history of recurrent rhabdomyolysis or malignant hyperthermia, sickle cell trait, or an episode after only trivial exertion.

Counselling that actually prevents the next episode: acclimatise gradually to heat and to new exercise; do not "make up" missed training with a punishment session; hydrate; avoid training through a febrile illness; be wary of pre-workout supplements and stimulants; and if the patient has sickle cell trait, treat maximal unaccustomed exertion in the heat as genuinely dangerous.

When clinic isn't enough

🚑

Red flags → send to the ED today

Send to the ED now if ANY of the following:
  • K⁺ ≥5.5 mEq/L — or any ECG change of hyperkalemia at any potassium level. This is the thing that kills first.
  • Any acute rise in creatinine (≥0.3 mg/dL above baseline, or ≥1.5× baseline — i.e. KDIGO Stage 1 AKI or worse).
  • CK >15,000–20,000 U/L, or a CK that is not falling on recheck.
  • McMahon score ≥6.
  • Dark urine with reduced urine output, or frank oliguria/anuria.
  • A tense, swollen, disproportionately painful limb, pain on passive stretch, or new paraesthesiae — possible compartment syndrome. This is a surgical emergency and cannot wait for a clinic recheck.
  • Crush injury or a prolonged "long lie" — regardless of how well the patient looks. These patients decompensate at reperfusion.
  • Bicarbonate <19, symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, perioral numbness, carpopedal spasm, seizure), or phosphate >5.4.
  • Hyperthermia with rigidity or agitation — think NMS, serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, heat stroke. These need active cooling and specific antidotes, not a follow-up appointment.
  • Cannot maintain oral intake, or unreliable for follow-up.
When you send them, say the word. "Rhabdomyolysis with a CK of 24,000 and a potassium of 5.9" gets triaged very differently from "muscle pain." Phone the ED, give the CK, the potassium, the creatinine and the urine output — and say whether you are worried about the limb.
