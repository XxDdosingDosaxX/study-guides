Penicillin allergy

⚠️

The most consequential wrong label in medicine

About 10% of the population carries a penicillin-allergy label, and more than 90–95% of them are not allergic. Some never were — the "rash" was the viral exanthem that brought them to the doctor in the first place, or an ampicillin rash during EBV infection. Others were, but IgE wanes: roughly half of genuinely IgE-sensitised patients lose reactivity by 5 years and about 80% by 10 years.

That label is not harmless. Patients labelled penicillin-allergic receive more vancomycin, fluoroquinolones and clindamycin; they have measurably more C. difficile, more MRSA and VRE, longer stays, and worse surgical-site infection rates. Delabelling is an intervention, not a formality.

Use a cephalosporin with a dissimilar R1 side chain (cefazolin, ceftriaxone, cefepime — all safe), a carbapenem (<1% cross-reactivity), or aztreonam. Refer to allergy for testing.
Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS, AGEP, serum sickness, interstitial nephritis, haemolytic anaemiaA severe non-IgE reaction.Absolute contraindication to the whole β-lactam class — never test, never challenge, never desensitise. Use a structurally unrelated class entirely.
Cross-reactivity is about the side chain, not the ring. The old "10% cross-reactivity with cephalosporins" figure came from 1960s–70s studies using cephalosporins contaminated with penicillin during manufacture. Modern data put it at roughly 1–2% or less, and the risk tracks R1 side-chain similarity. Amoxicillin shares an R1 side chain with cefadroxil, cefprozil and cefatrizine; ampicillin shares one with cephalexin, cefaclor and cephradine. Those are the cephalosporins to avoid. Cefazolin has a unique side chain shared with no penicillin and no other cephalosporin — it is the safest cephalosporin in penicillin allergy and can be given even to many patients with documented penicillin anaphylaxis.

PEN-FAST — is this penicillin allergy label safe to challenge?

A validated bedside rule for non-allergists. Entry criterion: the patient reports a penicillin allergy.

Five years or less since the reaction +2
Anaphylaxis or angioedema, or Severe cutaneous adverse reaction +2
Treatment was required for the reaction +1
Bands
0 — very low risk (~<1% positive)
1–2 — low risk (~5%)
3 — moderate risk (~20%)
4–5 — high risk (~50%)
A score <3 carries a negative predictive value of roughly 96% for a genuinely positive penicillin allergy test.
0
PEN-FAST identifies who can go straight to a direct oral amoxicillin challenge without skin testing. It does not replace allergy referral for high scores, and it does not apply to severe cutaneous adverse reactions, which are an absolute contraindication regardless of score.

Diabetes mellitusImpaired neutrophil function and microvascular disease → more severe and more polymicrobial infection. Diabetic foot infection is classically mixed aerobic gram-positive + gram-negative + anaerobe. Higher MRSA carriage. Higher risk of emphysematous pyelonephritis/cholecystitis, malignant otitis externa (Pseudomonas), and mucormycosis in DKA. Macrolides and fluoroquinolones prolong the QT in a population already on amiodarone/sotalol — check a baseline ECG.
Advanced age (>65)Blunted fever and leukocytosis; delirium may be the only presenting sign; higher rates of resistant organisms from healthcare exposure.Creatinine overestimates GFR when muscle mass is low — calculate CrCl, don't eyeball the creatinine. Highest C. difficile risk. Avoid fluoroquinolones (delirium, tendon rupture, aortic events) where an alternative exists. Watch cefepime neurotoxicity.
Neutropenia / immunosuppressionFever may be the only sign; classic findings (infiltrate, pus, meningismus) may be absent because there are no neutrophils to make them. Viridans strep, Pseudomonas and fungi become real threats.Bactericidal, antipseudomonal, and immediate — antibiotics within an hour of the first fever. Do not wait for a source. Add antifungal coverage for persistent fever at 4–7 days.
Seizure disorder—Meropenem drops valproate levels by 60–100% and has caused breakthrough status epilepticus — avoid the combination. Imipenem has the highest intrinsic seizure risk; cefepime and fluoroquinolones lower the seizure threshold.
Myasthenia gravis—Avoid fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, macrolides and telithromycin — all can precipitate a myasthenic crisis by impairing neuromuscular transmission. β-lactams are safe.
On warfarin—TMP-SMX (CYP2C9 inhibition + protein displacement) and metronidazole are the most dangerous; fluoroquinolones, macrolides and azoles also raise the INR. Any antibiotic that wipes gut flora reduces vitamin K₂. Check the INR within 3–5 days of starting and consider a pre-emptive dose reduction. Conversely, rifampin and nafcillin induce metabolism and cause loss of anticoagulation.
Transplant / calcineurin inhibitorsAtypical and opportunistic pathogens; blunted inflammatory response.Rifampin destroys tacrolimus/cyclosporine levels and can precipitate rejection. Clarithromycin and azoles do the opposite — they raise levels toward toxicity. Coordinate with transplant pharmacy before any of these.
G6PD deficiency—Avoid TMP-SMX, nitrofurantoin, dapsone and primaquine — oxidative haemolysis.
Prosthetic joint / device / hardwareBiofilm makes organisms phenotypically tolerant to almost every antibiotic; cure often requires hardware removal.Add rifampin 300–450 mg PO BID for staphylococcal infection with retained hardware (biofilm penetration) — always with a companion drug, never alone. Prolonged therapy and surgical planning are the rule.
ObesityHigher SSTI and surgical-site infection rates.Under-dosing is the commonest error: cefazolin 2–3 g (not 1 g), vancomycin loading on actual body weight, aminoglycosides on adjusted body weight. Fixed-dose agents (ertapenem, ceftriaxone) may under-perform at extremes of weight.
PregnancyAsymptomatic bacteriuria must be treated (unlike in non-pregnant adults) — untreated it progresses to pyelonephritis and preterm labour.Safe: penicillins, cephalosporins, azithromycin, clindamycin, metronidazole (after the first trimester by convention). Avoid: fluoroquinolones (cartilage), tetracyclines (teeth/bone, maternal hepatotoxicity), TMP-SMX (neural-tube defects in the first trimester; kernicterus near term), aminoglycosides (fetal ototoxicity). Nitrofurantoin is acceptable in the second trimester but avoided at term and in the first trimester where alternatives exist.
Drugs to avoid — the list worth committing to memory.
  • Daptomycin for pneumonia — inactivated by surfactant. It will fail.
  • Vancomycin for MSSA — inferior to cefazolin/nafcillin; de-escalate the moment you know.
  • Ceftriaxone for Enterobacter/Serratia/Citrobacter — AmpC de-repression and mid-course relapse.
  • Nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis or bacteraemia — no tissue or serum levels.
  • Moxifloxacin for UTI — does not reach therapeutic urinary concentrations.
  • Ciprofloxacin for cellulitis or pneumonia — no meaningful streptococcal or pneumococcal activity.
  • Tigecycline for bacteraemia, pneumonia or UTI — boxed mortality warning and inadequate serum/urine levels.
  • Cephalexin or clindamycin alone for an animal bite — both miss Pasteurella.
  • Any cephalosporin for Enterococcus or Listeria — intrinsic resistance.
  • Meropenem with valproate — breakthrough seizures.
  • Rifampin as monotherapy — resistance emerges within days.
  • Metronidazole added to pip-tazo, a carbapenem, or cefoxitin — redundant anaerobic coverage with pure added toxicity.

Creatinine clearance (Cockcroft-Gault) — the number antibiotic dosing actually uses

Nearly every antibiotic renal-dosing table is built on Cockcroft-Gault CrCl in mL/min, not on the eGFR your lab reports. They are not interchangeable — eGFR is normalised to 1.73 m² body surface area, which systematically misestimates dosing requirements at the extremes of body size.

—
CrCl = [(140 − age) × weight] ÷ (72 × Cr) × 0.85 if female. Use actual body weight in normal-weight patients; many pharmacies use adjusted body weight if >30% over ideal.

C. difficile risk by agent

Every antibiotic can cause C. difficile, but the risk is far from uniform. The pattern tracks how thoroughly the drug wipes out protective anaerobic colonic flora.

Risk tierAgentsWhy
HighestClindamycin; fluoroquinolones; 3rd- and 4th-generation cephalosporins; carbapenemsProfound and prolonged disruption of colonic anaerobes with no activity against C. difficile itself. Fluoroquinolone use drove the epidemic NAP1/BI/027 strain.
IntermediateBroad-spectrum penicillins (amox-clav, pip-tazo); 1st- and 2nd-generation cephalosporins; macrolidesMeaningful flora disruption but less complete.
LowestDoxycycline/tetracyclines; TMP-SMX; aminoglycosides; nitrofurantoin; IV vancomycin; metronidazole; daptomycin; linezolidLittle colonic anaerobic disruption; some (doxycycline, metronidazole) have direct anti-clostridial activity.
Risk is driven more by days of therapy and by number of agents than by any single drug choice, and remains elevated for around three months after the course ends. The most effective C. difficile prevention available to you is stopping antibiotics on time.
