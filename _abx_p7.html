🩺 Clinic — outpatient infections, oral regimens & when it is safe to stay home
Assess

Does this patient need an antibiotic at all?

🔍

The first and most important question in ambulatory infectious disease

Roughly a third of outpatient antibiotic prescriptions in the US are unnecessary, and most of that is respiratory. Before you write anything, decide whether this is bacterial, whether it will resolve without treatment, and whether the harm of treating exceeds the benefit.

PresentationAntibiotic?Reasoning
Acute bronchitis / cough <3 weeks, no COPDNo>90% viral. Purulent sputum reflects sloughed inflammatory cells, not bacterial infection. Antibiotics shorten cough by less than a day and cause harm in roughly 1 in 5.
Upper respiratory infection / "sinus pressure" <10 daysNoViral rhinosinusitis peaks at 3–6 days and resolves. Treat only with ≥10 days without improvement, severe symptoms (fever ≥39 °C + purulent discharge) for ≥3–4 days, or "double worsening" — improvement then deterioration.
Sore throat, Centor/McIsaac 0–1NoTest only if 2–3 points; treat only if the rapid antigen test or culture is positive. Antibiotics for strep pharyngitis exist chiefly to prevent acute rheumatic fever, not to shorten symptoms.
Asymptomatic bacteriuriaNoTreating it does not prevent symptomatic UTI and does cause resistance and C. difficile. Exceptions: pregnancy and before an invasive urologic procedure with expected mucosal bleeding. Do not send a urine culture in a patient with no urinary symptoms.
Chronic leg swelling, bilateral, no feverUsually noBilateral "cellulitis" is almost always stasis dermatitis or lipodermatosclerosis. True cellulitis is nearly always unilateral. Around a third of admitted "cellulitis" is a mimic.
Simple cutaneous abscess, drainedOften yes nowIncision and drainage is the treatment; two randomised trials (Talan 2016 and Daum 2017) showed adding TMP-SMX or clindamycin improves cure by roughly 7–10 percentage points, so most patients should get an oral agent.
Uncomplicated diverticulitis, immunocompetent, wellOften noThe AVOD and DIABOLO randomised trials found no benefit of antibiotics; guidelines now support selective, not routine, use in mild disease with reliable follow-up.

Delayed or "safety-net" prescribing — giving the patient a prescription with instructions to fill it only if they are not better in 2–3 days — reduces antibiotic use substantially without increasing complications in sinusitis, otitis and bronchitis. It is often the right compromise when a patient expects a prescription and the clinical picture does not justify one.

Non-purulent cellulitis. Unilateral, spreading, warm erythema with indistinct borders. No fluctuance, no drainage — this is a streptococcal disease, and empiric MRSA coverage is not indicated.

Non-purulent cellulitis / erysipelas — target: β-haemolytic streptococci

Non-purulent cellulitis is overwhelmingly streptococcal, not staphylococcal. Adding MRSA coverage to a simple cellulitis does not improve cure rates and adds toxicity — a randomised trial of cephalexin versus cephalexin + TMP-SMX showed no difference. Cover strep, and cover staph only if there is pus.

With comorbidities — chronic heart, lung, liver or renal disease; diabetes; alcohol use disorder; malignancy; asplenia; or antibiotics in the last 90 daysAmox-clav 875/125 mg PO BID (or 2000/125 mg BID) or a cephalosporin (cefpodoxime 200 mg BID / cefuroxime 500 mg BID) PLUS a macrolide or doxycycline 100 mg BIDRespiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy: levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily or moxifloxacin 400 mg PO daily3–5 daysThe β-lactam covers pneumococcus, H. influenzae and MSSA; the macrolide/doxycycline covers Mycoplasma, Legionella, Chlamydia. A respiratory quinolone covers both in one drug — reserve it, because of the boxed warnings and C. difficile risk.
CAP duration just got shorter. The 2019 ATS/IDSA guideline set a floor of no less than 5 days, continued until clinical stability. The 2026 ATS guideline (Jones et al., Am J Respir Crit Care Med 2026;212:24–44, published online July 2025) now suggests less than 5 days — with a minimum of 3 — for outpatients and for non-severe inpatients once they have reached clinical stability (both conditional recommendations, low-certainty evidence). For severe CAP it recommends ≥5 days, and that one is a strong recommendation. The evidence base is the PTC trial, in which 3 days of β-lactam was non-inferior to 8 in patients stable at 72 hours (cure 77% vs 68%). Note that the panel itself was split on the non-severe-inpatient recommendation (69% consensus) — so stability, not the calendar, is what licenses stopping.
The 2019 ATS/IDSA guideline abandoned the term "healthcare-associated pneumonia" (HCAP) — it over-predicted resistant organisms and drove enormous unnecessary vancomycin and pip-tazo use. Cover MRSA or Pseudomonas only for prior isolation of that organism, or hospitalisation with parenteral antibiotics within 90 days plus locally validated risk factors.

Nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin and moxifloxacin have no role in pyelonephritis — none of them achieves adequate renal tissue concentrations.
Outpatient pyelonephritis is safe when the patient is haemodynamically stable, can keep down oral fluids and medication, is not pregnant, has no obstruction (no stone, no hydronephrosis, no indwelling hardware), is not severely immunocompromised, and has follow-up within 48–72 hours. Anything else — vomiting, hypotension, obstruction, sepsis, pregnancy, or male sex with suspected prostatitis needing prolonged therapy — goes inpatient.
Men, catheters and recurrence. UTI in a man is by definition complicated; consider prostatitis and use an agent that penetrates prostate (fluoroquinolone or TMP-SMX) for 10–14 days (up to 6 weeks in chronic bacterial prostatitis). For catheter-associated UTI, exchange or remove the catheter before or at the start of therapy — otherwise you are treating a biofilm you cannot reach.
