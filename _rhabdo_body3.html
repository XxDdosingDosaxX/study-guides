Who to admit

📋

Admission criteria — the numbers

There is no validated admission rule for rhabdomyolysis. What follows is the pragmatic synthesis used in practice, anchored to the risk factors that the McMahon model and the crush literature actually identified.

Admit if…Why
CK >5,000 U/L (and essentially always if >15,000)Meaningfully raised AKI risk; needs IV fluid and serial chemistry. The UAB trauma protocol triggers formal treatment at CK >10,000; most physicians admit and hydrate above 5,000.
McMahon score ≥6Not low risk for death or dialysis — even if the CK looks unimpressive. This is the single best admission discriminator you have.
Any AKI — creatinine ≥1.5× baseline or ≥0.3 mg/dL rise (KDIGO ≥Stage 1)The kidney is already injured; the therapeutic window is closing.
K⁺ ≥5.5 mEq/L, or any hyperkalemic ECG changeThe early killer. Needs monitored treatment and serial potassiums.
Bicarbonate <19 mEq/L, phosphate >5.4 mg/dL, or calcium <7.5 mg/dLAll three are McMahon variables — each marks a sicker patient.
Crush injury, entrapment, or a prolonged "long lie"Highest-risk aetiology. These patients look deceptively well and then crash at reperfusion. Admit regardless of the initial CK.
Oliguria, or dark urine that has not clearedOngoing myoglobin load with inadequate flow.
A trigger you cannot switch off — ongoing seizures, agitation, hyperthermia, sepsis, ischaemic limbMuscle is still dying. Fluid alone will not keep pace.
Significant comorbidity — CKD, heart failure, cirrhosis, age >65Less reserve, and the treatment itself (litres of fluid) is hazardous. These patients need supervised resuscitation.
Cannot drink, or unreliable for follow-upOral hydration is not a plan if it will not happen.

💧

Fluid, subtraction, surveillance

__SVG_mgmtdark__
__SVG_mgmtlight__
The rhabdomyolysis algorithm end-to-end. Note that the CK number routes the patient, but the McMahon score rescues the middle band (CK 1,000–5,000) from being under-treated — and that compartment syndrome is a parallel track you must check continuously, not once.
1
Fluid — early, isotonic, and titrated to urine output
The only therapy with a real effect size
▾
Fluid attacks two of the three mechanisms of AKI at once: it refills the intravascular space the muscle stole (reversing renal vasoconstriction) and it generates a torrent of dilute urine that keeps myoglobin below its precipitation threshold and physically flushes casts out of the tubules. Nothing else in this disease does that.

Timing is the whole ballgame. The Israeli crush-injury experience (Better & Abassi) established that fluid started at the scene — even before extrication — prevents AKI, while fluid started after the patient reaches hospital largely does not. The DASAIM/DSIT guideline formalises this as its first recommendation: early rather than late fluid resuscitation (weak recommendation, very low quality evidence — because nobody has ever randomised a crush victim, and nobody will).

Targets:

  • Urine output 200–300 mL/h (the crush-syndrome target), or 1–2 mL/kg/h (the UAB trauma protocol's target). Both are roughly 2–3× a normal urine output — that is the point.
  • Expect to give large volumes: the ISN crush target is 3–6 L of isotonic saline within the first 6 hours of contact, and daily totals of 6–12 L are described in severe crush. Non-crush rhabdomyolysis usually needs far less.
  • Continue until the CK is reliably down-trending and the urine has cleared. Do not chase the CK to normal — that takes many days, and by then the myoglobin is long gone.
The counter-error is real and it kills too. "Go big or go home" is not a licence to drown people. Blind, protocolised high-rate fluid in an oliguric patient — especially one with CKD or heart failure — produces pulmonary oedema and compartment syndrome. Fluid is titrated to a measured urine output, reassessed at least hourly at first. If the patient is not making urine despite adequate resuscitation, the answer is not more fluid — it is nephrology and dialysis.

Fluids — pick the agent, the rate, and the endpoint

💉

The full fluid ladder — start → titrate → target → stop

FluidStart → titration → target/maxWhy it worksWatch for / alternative
0.9% saline
(isotonic saline)		 Start: crush/severe — 1,000 mL/h in adults (children 15–20 mL/kg), started before extrication if possible.
Titrate: if extrication takes >2 h, reduce to ≤500 mL/h. After extrication continue ~1,000 mL/h.
Target: 3–6 L in the first 6 h of contact; then titrate to urine output 200–300 mL/h (or 1–2 mL/kg/h).
Stop/step down: when CK is reliably falling and urine has cleared — or immediately if the patient is oliguric and becoming overloaded.		 Restores intravascular volume, reverses renal afferent vasoconstriction, and drives the high urine flow that dilutes myoglobin and washes out casts. The ISN crush guideline's preferred solution — precisely because it contains no potassium. Large volumes cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis — which acidifies the urine, the exact opposite of what you want for myoglobin solubility. Watch the chloride and the bicarbonate; consider switching to a balanced fluid once the potassium is known to be safe.
Lactated Ringer's
/ Plasma-Lyte
(balanced crystalloid)		 Start / titrate / target: as for saline above — the rates are the same; only the solute differs. Avoids hyperchloremic acidosis; its lactate/acetate is metabolised to bicarbonate, which mildly alkalinises the urine — theoretically helpful for keeping myoglobin soluble. Favoured by many critical-care sources for non-crush rhabdomyolysis. ⚠ It contains ~4 mmol/L of potassium. The ISN crush recommendations say to avoid potassium-containing solutions in crush victims. Resolve the conflict this way: if the patient is hyperkalemic, anuric, or a crush victim → 0.9% saline. If potassium is normal and the kidneys are making urine → balanced crystalloid is reasonable and arguably better.
Isotonic sodium bicarbonate
(e.g. 150 mEq NaHCO₃ in 1 L D5W)		 Start: only in selected patients (see below). Substitute for part of the crystalloid.
Titrate to: urine pH >6.5.
STOP if: urine pH does not rise after ~4 h; serum pH >7.5 or serum bicarbonate >30; or symptomatic hypocalcemia develops.		 Alkaline urine keeps myoglobin soluble (less Tamm-Horsfall co-precipitation) and blunts heme-driven free-radical chemistry — hitting mechanisms #2 and #3. Also treats the metabolic acidosis and helps shift potassium. The theory is beautiful; the evidence is not. Brown (J Trauma 2004) — 2,083 trauma ICU patients — found that among those with CK >5,000, bicarbonate + mannitol produced no difference in renal failure, dialysis, or mortality. DASAIM/DSIT 2019 suggests AGAINST routine bicarbonate and against routine alkalinisation. Alkalinisation also drives calcium into cells and worsens hypocalcemia — a genuine harm. Reasonable use: a patient with a significant metabolic acidosis, where you are correcting the acidosis anyway. Not as a reflex.
Mannitol 20% Test dose first: 60 mL of 20% mannitol IV over 3–5 min.
Continue only if urine output rises by 30–50 mL/h above baseline; stop if there is no significant increase.
If continued: 1–2 g/kg/day, max ~120 g/day, infused at ~5 g/h (ISN crush protocol: 50 mL of 20% mannitol added to each litre of half-normal saline + 50 mmol bicarbonate).		 An osmotic diuretic: expands intravascular volume, forces a diuresis that flushes casts, scavenges free radicals, and may reduce intracompartmental pressure. ⚠ Largely abandoned, and rightly so. Contraindicated in anuria (it will simply accumulate and cause hyperosmolarity and its own nephrotoxicity). Brown 2004: no benefit. DASAIM/DSIT 2019: suggests AGAINST use. The UAB protocol: "should not be used." Retains a toehold only in some crush protocols where close monitoring is possible. Do not reach for it on the wards.
Loop diuretics
(furosemide, bumetanide)		 Not for treating rhabdomyolysis. Use only to manage established volume overload in an already-resuscitated patient — e.g. furosemide 20–40 mg IV (double the dose if no response; higher in CKD). Produces urine — but urine volume is not the goal in itself; renal perfusion is. DASAIM/DSIT suggests against routine diuretics. They can worsen hypovolemia (making AKI worse) and they acidify the urine, favouring cast formation. Giving furosemide to an under-resuscitated oliguric rhabdomyolysis patient is a classic and harmful error.
Albumin / hydroxyethyl starch
(colloids)		 Do not use for volume resuscitation. — The ISN crush guideline explicitly says not to select colloids for volume resuscitation in crush victims (no mortality benefit, more side effects, higher cost). DASAIM/DSIT: crystalloids preferred over colloids. HES additionally carries a nephrotoxicity signal.
The honest summary of 50 years of rhabdomyolysis research — and the one place the evidence is actually strong.

Two GRADE-based society guidelines, written on different continents from different literature sets, reached the same three conclusions:

  • EAST 2022 (Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma, Am J Surg) — conditionally FOR aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (it reduces acute renal failure and the need for dialysis); conditionally AGAINST bicarbonate; conditionally AGAINST mannitol.
  • DASAIM/DSIT 2019 (Acta Anaesthesiol Scand) — FOR early rather than late fluid, and crystalloid over colloid; AGAINST routine bicarbonate, alkalinisation, mannitol, diuretics, and routine RRT. All eight recommendations weak, on low/very low quality evidence.

That independent convergence is the strongest evidentiary statement this topic has. If you remember one thing from this section: give isotonic fluid, give it early, titrate it to urine output, and don't add anything clever.

Electrolyte emergencies

⚡

Hyperkalemia — the thing that kills in the first hours

In rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia is not a lab abnormality — it is the leading early cause of death. Necrotic muscle is the body's biggest potassium reservoir, the kidney that should excrete it is failing, and the acidosis is pushing still more K⁺ out of cells. In crush injury the release is classically abrupt at reperfusion — the victim who is talking during extrication and arrests the moment the slab is lifted. Get the ECG before you get the potassium.
Hyperkalemia. Tall, narrow, symmetrically peaked T waves — the earliest change. As K⁺ climbs: the PR lengthens and P waves flatten and disappear → the QRS widens → it merges with the T into a sine wave → asystole/VF. The ECG, not the number, dictates urgency: a patient with a widened QRS at K⁺ 6.2 is in more danger than one with peaked T waves at 7.0.

The hyperkalemia ladder — stabilise, shift, then remove

DrugStart → repeat/titration → maxOnset / durationWhy it worksWatch for / alternative
① STABILISE the myocardium — if there are ECG changes, or K⁺ ≥6.5
Calcium gluconate 10% Start: 10 mL (1 g) IV over 2–3 min.
Repeat every 5–10 min if ECG changes persist — no real maximum when the ECG demands it; titrate to ECG normalisation.		 Onset 1–3 min; duration only 30–60 min Raises the myocyte threshold potential back away from the (elevated) resting potential, restoring the gap that lets the heart depolarise normally. It lowers the potassium by exactly zero — it buys you 30–60 minutes to actually shift and remove the K⁺. Give it and keep going. Calcium chloride 10% 10 mL contains ~3× the elemental calcium — use it in arrest or if peripheral gluconate is failing, but give it centrally if you can (it is a vesicant and causes tissue necrosis if it extravasates).
Digoxin toxicity: the old teaching to withhold calcium is largely discredited, but give it slowly, diluted, if that is the setting.
② SHIFT potassium into cells — buys hours, removes nothing
Insulin (regular) + dextrose Start: 10 units regular insulin IV with 25 g dextrose (50 mL of D50) IV.
Reduce to 5 units if AKI/CKD or the patient is small/frail — impaired insulin clearance makes hypoglycaemia much more likely.
If glucose >250 mg/dL: give the insulin without the dextrose.
Repeat q4–6 h as needed.		 Onset 10–20 min; peak 30–60 min; lasts 4–6 h Insulin drives the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — the same pump that failed in the dying myocyte — pushing K⁺ from the extracellular space back into cells. The most reliable shifting agent; lowers K⁺ by roughly 0.5–1.2 mEq/L. Hypoglycaemia is the commonest iatrogenic harm on the wards. Check glucose at 30 min, then hourly for at least 6 h — the insulin outlasts the dextrose bolus. Consider a D10 infusion after the bolus in high-risk patients.
Albuterol (salbutamol), nebulised 10–20 mg nebulised over 10–15 min — note this is 4–8× the standard bronchodilator dose (a normal 2.5 mg neb will not do this job).
May repeat.		 Onset ~30 min; lasts 2–4 h β₂-agonism stimulates the same Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase by a different (cAMP) route — so its effect is additive to insulin. Lowers K⁺ by ~0.5–1 mEq/L. Never use as monotherapy — up to ~40% of patients are non-responders. Causes tachycardia and tremor; use cautiously in active coronary ischaemia.
Sodium bicarbonate Only if there is a metabolic acidosis — e.g. as part of an isotonic bicarbonate infusion. Slow, unreliable Raising pH shifts K⁺ intracellularly in exchange for H⁺. A poor acute potassium-lowering agent on its own — do not rely on it in an emergency. And in rhabdomyolysis it carries a specific cost: it worsens hypocalcemia.
③ REMOVE potassium from the body — the only definitive step
Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC) 10 g PO three times daily for up to 48 h, then 10 g once daily (maintenance range 5–15 g daily). Onset ~1 h; meaningful effect by 4–6 h A non-absorbed crystalline lattice that selectively traps K⁺ in the gut lumen in exchange for Na⁺/H⁺, so it is excreted in stool. Actually removes potassium, unlike everything in step ②. Sodium load may matter in heart failure. Can cause oedema. Not fast enough for a crashing patient — it is an adjunct, not a rescue.
Patiromer 8.4 g PO once daily; titrate by 8.4 g at ≥1-week intervals to max 25.2 g daily. Onset ~7 h — too slow for an emergency Binds K⁺ in the colon in exchange for calcium; excreted in stool. Separate from other oral drugs by ≥3 h (binds them). Causes hypomagnesaemia and constipation. A chronic agent — not an acute one.
Sodium polystyrene sulfonate
(Kayexalate / SPS)		 15–30 g PO. Slow, unpredictable Cation-exchange resin. Largely superseded — weak evidence, and a real risk of colonic necrosis (especially with sorbitol, and in post-operative or ileus patients). Exception: the ISN crush guidance recommends pre-emptive Kayexalate during transport in mass-disaster settings — where there is no lab, no monitor, and no dialysis, so an imperfect drug beats nothing.
Loop diuretic Furosemide 20–40 mg IV (higher in CKD). Onset ~30 min if kidneys respond Increases distal sodium delivery → renal K⁺ excretion. Only in a patient who is volume-replete and making urine. Useless in oliguric AKI, and harmful if they are still hypovolemic.
Haemodialysis The definitive therapy. Call for it early — do not wait for the medical ladder to fail twice. Immediate and reliable Physically removes potassium from the blood. This is the specific rhabdomyolysis point: because necrotic muscle keeps releasing potassium for days, the shifting agents in step ② wear off and the K⁺ rebounds — repeatedly. A patient who needs insulin/albuterol three times in 12 hours does not have a medication problem; they have a dialysis indication. Escalate early if myonecrosis is ongoing.

Calcium & phosphate — where good intentions do harm

ProblemWhat to doWhy — and why the obvious move is wrong
Hypocalcemia (early, asymptomatic) Do NOT treat. Observe and re-measure. The single most counter-intuitive rule in this disease. That calcium has not vanished — it has precipitated with phosphate into the damaged muscle, and during recovery it will be mobilised back out, causing rebound hypercalcemia in a substantial minority of patients. Giving calcium now front-loads that rebound and promotes metastatic calcification in muscle, vessels and kidney. It may also worsen the intracellular calcium overload that is killing the myocytes in the first place.
Hypocalcemia — symptomatic (tetany, carpopedal spasm, seizures, laryngospasm) or needed for hyperkalemic cardiac stabilisation Treat without hesitation. Calcium gluconate 10% 10 mL (1 g) IV over 2–3 min; repeat as needed. Symptomatic hypocalcemia and a hyperkalemic myocardium will kill the patient today; rebound hypercalcemia is a problem for next week. The rule is "don't treat asymptomatic hypocalcemia" — it is not "never give calcium." Do not let a memorised aphorism stop you stabilising a heart.
Hyperphosphatemia Treat the underlying process; oral phosphate binders (e.g. calcium acetate, sevelamer) if marked and persistent. Dialysis if severe with AKI. Released from myocytes and retained by the failing kidney. Drives the hypocalcemia (calcium-phosphate precipitation) and contributes to metastatic calcification. Prefer a non-calcium binder (sevelamer) here, precisely because you are trying not to load calcium.
Rebound hypercalcemia (recovery phase, ~day 3–10) Usually just stop giving calcium and hydrate. Treat actively only if severe or symptomatic. Calcium deposited in muscle is released as the muscle heals, and calcitriol rebounds. Its severity is proportional to how much calcium you gave during the hypocalcemic phase — which is the whole reason for the restraint above.
Hyperuricemia Usually needs no specific therapy; fluid handles it. Rasburicase is not routine here. Purines from lysed myocytes → urate. Contributes a crystal load to an already-injured tubule, but volume expansion is the treatment.
Metabolic acidosis (anion gap) Treat the cause (perfusion, the ongoing necrosis). Bicarbonate if severe — see the fluid table for the caveats. Organic acids from necrotic muscle plus failing renal acid excretion. Acidosis worsens hyperkalemia and favours myoglobin cast formation — so it is not just a number; it actively feeds the disease.

Escalate

When to call the ICU

⬆️

Escalation triggers — with numbers

Transfer to the ICU for any of:
  • K⁺ ≥6.5 mEq/L, any hyperkalemic ECG change (peaked T waves with QRS widening, loss of P waves, sine wave), or a potassium that rebounds after treatment — because ongoing myonecrosis will keep re-loading it.
  • Refractory hyperkalemia requiring repeated shifting therapy, or requiring urgent dialysis.
  • Oliguria/anuria despite adequate volume resuscitation — i.e. established AKI needing RRT. (The UAB protocol prompts a nephrology call when CK stays high and fluid exceeds 3 L/24 h without response.)
  • Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.2, or bicarbonate falling despite therapy).
  • Compartment syndrome — or a limb you are worried enough about to be measuring pressures. Delta pressure ≤30 mmHg → theatre.
  • Haemodynamic instability — hypotension not corrected by fluid, or needing vasopressors.
  • Volume overload with respiratory compromise — a real and common consequence of correct treatment in the wrong kidney.
  • DIC, or any evidence of multi-organ failure.
  • Hyperthermia with rigidity — malignant hyperthermia, NMS, or serotonin syndrome needing active cooling and antidote.
  • CK >50,000–100,000 with any organ dysfunction, or a CK that continues to climb past 72 h despite treatment.
Escalate on the trajectory, not the snapshot. A potassium of 6.0 that has fallen from 7.2 is a patient who is being treated well. A potassium of 6.0 that was 5.2 four hours ago, in a patient whose CK is still climbing, is a patient whose muscle is still dying — and they will be at 7.5 by midnight. The second patient goes to the unit.
Dispo

Discharge & disposition

🏠

Readiness criteria, then where they actually go

Discharge readiness — all of the following:

  • CK clearly and consistently down-trending (it does not need to be normal — that takes many days, and by then the myoglobin is long gone).
  • Renal function stable or improving; not requiring dialysis, or established on an outpatient dialysis plan.
  • Potassium normal and stable off shifting therapy — this is the one that catches people out. A potassium held down by q6h insulin is not a stable potassium.
  • Calcium, phosphate and bicarbonate acceptable; no symptomatic hypocalcemia; no rising rebound hypercalcemia.
  • Adequate urine output off IV fluids, and able to maintain oral intake.
  • The cause has been identified and addressed — statin stopped, alcohol/withdrawal addressed, thyroid treated, seizures controlled, compartment decompressed.
  • Compartment syndrome excluded or definitively treated; wounds/fasciotomy sites have a plan.
  • Mobilising safely, and a follow-up CK/BMP is booked (typically within ~1 week).

Home

Independent, CK falling, renal function and electrolytes stable off IV therapy, cause fixed, reliable for follow-up. The majority of exertional and statin cases.

Home health

Home but needs nursing for wound/fasciotomy care, or lab draws, or help with mobility. Common after a fasciotomy that is healing by secondary intention.

SNF

Deconditioned — often the older "long lie" patient — needing 24 h skilled nursing but tolerating <3 h of therapy/day. Also for wound care that the family cannot manage.

Acute rehab (IRF)

Can tolerate ≥3 h therapy/day across ≥2 disciplines. Typical after crush injury or fasciotomy with substantial functional loss but good rehab potential.

LTAC

Prolonged complex care — persistent dialysis dependence with other organ support, ventilator weaning, or complex multi-site wounds, expected >25 days.

Outpatient dialysis

Renal recovery is the usual outcome after rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI — most patients who need RRT recover independent renal function. But some leave dialysis-dependent and need a permanent access plan and a nephrologist.
The discharge conversation that prevents the readmission. Tell them, in plain words: "Your muscle broke down and it poisoned your kidneys. It can happen again." Then be concrete — name the trigger and how to avoid it; say which drug is never going back on; give the staged return-to-exercise plan if it was exertional; and tell them to come straight back if the urine goes dark again. Book the follow-up CK before they leave.
