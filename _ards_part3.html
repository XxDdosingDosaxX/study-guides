🩺 Clinic — catching it early, preventing it, and the life after it
Assess

Who is on the road to respiratory failure

🔍

The three clinic patients

ARDS does not start in clinic — but three quite different patients bring it to your clinic door, and conflating them is the commonest cognitive error in this disease.

PatientWhat is actually going onYour job
1. The patient developing acute failure right now
"Doctor, I've been more short of breath for two days"		Early pneumonia, influenza/COVID, decompensated heart failure, PE, or an asthma/COPD exacerbation — any of which can be 24–48 h from ARDS.Do not manage this in clinic. Get a saturation on room air, count the respiratory rate yourself, and apply the red-flag list below. This patient's outcome is decided by how fast they get to an ED, not by anything you prescribe.
2. The patient with CHRONIC respiratory failure
COPD, ILD, obesity-hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease		Compensated type 1 or type 2 failure. The bicarbonate is high, the pH is normal — the kidney has had weeks to adapt.Long-term oxygen assessment, non-invasive ventilation assessment, vaccination, smoking cessation, pulmonary rehab, and an advance-care-planning conversation before the crisis.
3. The ARDS SURVIVOR
3 months post-discharge, "not right since"		Post-intensive-care syndrome (PICS) — the reason this section exists. See the survivorship module below. This is the patient most often failed by follow-up, because everyone assumes someone else is managing it.Screen deliberately for physical, cognitive and psychiatric sequelae. Most will not volunteer them.
Count the respiratory rate yourself, for a full 60 seconds. The number written in the chart is very often "18" — a number nobody measured. Respiratory rate is the earliest vital sign to deteriorate in respiratory failure and the last one anyone actually counts. An RR of 24 in a patient who says they feel "a bit breathless" is a genuinely abnormal finding, and it will be normal-looking on every monitor in your clinic.
SpO₂ has a lying problem you must know about. Pulse oximetry overestimates true arterial saturation in patients with darker skin pigmentation, with roughly three-fold higher rates of occult hypoxemia (a true SaO₂ < 88% while the oximeter reads 92–96%) in Black compared with White patients (Sjoding et al., NEJM 2020). It also reads falsely high in carbon monoxide poisoning (carboxyhaemoglobin is read as oxyhaemoglobin — the saturation can be 100% in a dying patient) and is unreliable in methaemoglobinaemia (drifts toward 85% regardless of the truth), severe anaemia, poor perfusion/vasoconstriction, motion, and nail polish. If the clinical picture and the oximeter disagree, believe the patient and get a blood gas.

Outpatient labs & imaging

Pulse oximetry — at rest AND walkingEvery dyspnoeic patient, every visit. The ambulatory desaturation test is the highest-yield thing you can do in a clinic room: walk them and re-check. Calculator — A–a gradient

The bedside tool that tells you where the oxygenation is failing: in the lung, or upstream of it.

Alveolar gas equation at sea level (Patm 760 mmHg, PH₂O 47 mmHg, respiratory quotient R = 0.8). Expected A–a gradient ≈ (age/4) + 4 on room air. At altitude, substitute the local barometric pressure — the equation, and this calculator, assume sea level.

Treat

Chronic respiratory failure — the outpatient ladder

💨

Stepwise therapy — first-line → escalation

1
Treat the underlying disease — and stop the smoking
The only two things that change the natural history.
▾

Chronic respiratory failure is always a complication of something. Optimise that something: GOLD-directed inhaled therapy for COPD, antifibrotics for IPF, guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, weight loss and CPAP for obesity-hypoventilation, immunosuppression for the relevant ILDs.

Smoking cessation is the single highest-yield intervention in the entire pulmonary clinic. It is the only thing that alters the slope of FEV₁ decline in COPD. Combining pharmacotherapy with behavioural support roughly doubles-to-triples quit rates versus either alone.

2
Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT)
The only oxygen prescription that has ever been shown to prolong life.
▾

The evidence base is the NOTT (1980) and MRC (1981) trials in COPD, and the qualifying thresholds below descend directly from them. They remain the criteria used for reimbursement.

Qualify if…Threshold (measured when clinically stable, on optimal therapy, ≥ 30–90 days after an exacerbation)
Severe resting hypoxaemiaPaO₂ ≤ 55 mmHg or SpO₂ ≤ 88%
Moderate hypoxaemia WITH evidence of end-organ effectPaO₂ 56–59 mmHg (or SpO₂ 89%) PLUS cor pulmonale / right heart failure, peripheral oedema, or polycythaemia (haematocrit > 55%)

The prescription

Dose: the lowest flow that achieves SpO₂ 90–92% (typically 1–3 L/min by nasal cannula). Duration is the active ingredient: ≥ 15 hours per day, including overnight. NOTT showed continuous (~19 h/day) oxygen roughly halved mortality versus nocturnal-only — the survival benefit is dose-dependent on hours worn. Titrate separately for exertion and for sleep (both often need more).

Chronic alveolar hypoxia causes hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction across the whole lung, driving pulmonary hypertension and, eventually, right heart failure (cor pulmonale). Sustained oxygen relieves that vasoconstriction, lowers pulmonary artery pressure and unloads the right ventricle. The mortality benefit is haemodynamic, not symptomatic — which is precisely why it depends on hours worn and why oxygen given only for breathlessness does nothing for survival.
Do not prescribe oxygen for breathlessness in a non-hypoxaemic patient. The LOTT trial (NEJM 2016) randomised COPD patients with moderate resting or exertional desaturation and found no benefit on mortality, hospitalisation, exacerbations, walk distance or quality of life. Oxygen is a drug with a specific indication — not a comfort measure. For refractory breathlessness without hypoxaemia, the evidence supports a handheld fan directed at the face (trigeminal afferents genuinely relieve air hunger) and, in advanced disease, low-dose opioids.
3
Home non-invasive ventilation (for chronic hypercapnia)
When the problem is the pump, not the gas exchanger.
▾

Oxygen fixes hypoxaemia. It does not fix hypercapnia — and in a CO₂-retaining patient it can worsen it. When the failure is ventilatory (type 2), the treatment is ventilation.

IndicationWhoTypical settings
COPD with chronic hypercapniaPersistent PaCO₂ ≥ 52 mmHg when clinically stable (i.e. re-measured ≥ 2–4 weeks after an exacerbation, not during one), or persistent hypercapnia after an episode of acute-on-chronic failure requiring NIV.High-intensity NIV: bilevel with a genuinely high IPAP, targeted at lowering the CO₂ — this is the key insight from the German trial (Köhnlein, Lancet Respir Med 2014) and from HOT-HMV (Murphy, JAMA 2017), which showed reduced readmission and mortality. Low, "comfortable" pressures do not work.
Obesity-hypoventilation syndromeBMI ≥ 30 + awake PaCO₂ ≥ 45 mmHg, with no other explanation.Most have concurrent OSA: start CPAP. Escalate to bilevel if hypercapnia or nocturnal desaturation persists on CPAP. Weight loss is the definitive therapy.
Neuromuscular disease / chest-wall disease (ALS, muscular dystrophy, kyphoscoliosis)Symptoms + any of: FVC < 50% predicted, maximal inspiratory pressure < 60 cmH₂O, nocturnal desaturation, or daytime PaCO₂ ≥ 45.Nocturnal bilevel. In ALS, NIV is one of the few interventions that prolongs survival and improves quality of life — start the conversation early, well before the FVC collapses.
4
Pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination & advance care planning
The unglamorous things with the best effect sizes.
▾

Pulmonary rehabilitation — a supervised exercise and education programme, typically 6–12 weeks. In COPD it improves dyspnoea, exercise capacity and quality of life with effect sizes that comfortably exceed any inhaler, and when delivered after an exacerbation it reduces readmission and mortality. It is also the core of ARDS-survivor recovery. It is drastically under-referred; refer.

Advance care planning — have the conversation about intubation and tracheostomy in clinic, when the patient can speak, not at 3 a.m. in an ED when they cannot. For progressive neuromuscular disease and end-stage COPD/IPF this is a core part of the treatment plan, not an afterthought. Document it where the ED will actually find it.

🌱

The ARDS survivor — post-intensive-care syndrome (PICS)

This module exists because ARDS survivorship is, systematically, the most neglected part of the disease. Survival has improved; the survivors have not been looked after. The landmark data come from Herridge's Toronto cohort (NEJM 2003 and 2011), which followed ARDS survivors for five years. The headline finding is counter-intuitive and worth memorising: at five years, pulmonary function had largely returned to normal — and yet the patients had not. Their exercise capacity and quality of life remained persistently and substantially reduced. The lasting disability of ARDS is neuromuscular, cognitive and psychiatric — it is not the lung.

DomainWhat survivors actually haveWhat to do in clinic
PhysicalICU-acquired weakness (a critical-illness polyneuromyopathy) in up to ~⅓ of ARDS survivors — profound proximal weakness, wasting, foot drop, contractures, and persistently reduced 6-minute walk distance years later. Also: heterotopic ossification, frozen shoulder, tracheal stenosis, and often chronic pain from cannulation and immobility.Refer to physical and occupational therapy and to pulmonary rehabilitation. Track objectively with a 6-minute walk test. Take a swallowing history — post-extubation dysphagia is common and is aspirated silently.
CognitiveNew, long-lasting deficits in memory, attention and executive function in a large proportion — in the BRAIN-ICU cohort (Pandharipande, NEJM 2013), global cognition at 12 months was, in many patients, comparable to mild Alzheimer's or moderate traumatic brain injury. The duration of ICU delirium is the strongest modifiable predictor.Screen (MoCA). Warn the patient and — crucially — the family, who typically interpret the deficits as personality change or laziness. Do not clear them to return to a cognitively demanding job or to driving without assessment.
PsychiatricDepression, anxiety and PTSD — each in roughly a quarter to a third of survivors, and often persisting for years. Frequently driven by delusional memories formed during sedation and delirium, which patients recall vividly and are often too embarrassed to mention.Ask explicitly — they will not raise it. Screen (PHQ-9, GAD-7, IES-R). Refer. An ICU diary, and simply walking the patient through what actually happened to them, has genuine evidence for reducing PTSD symptoms.
PulmonaryUsually the best news: most survivors have near-normal spirometry and lung volumes by 6–12 months, with a mildly reduced DLCO the commonest residual abnormality. Significant fibrosis is the exception, not the rule.PFTs with DLCO at ~6 months. Reassure — and then redirect your attention to the three domains above, which is where the actual disability lives.
FamilyPICS-F: the patient's family members have their own high rates of depression, anxiety and PTSD.Ask the caregiver how they are. Genuinely.
The single sentence to give every ARDS survivor at discharge: "Your lungs will very likely recover. Your strength, your memory and your mood will take much longer, and that is expected — it is not you failing." It reframes months of frightening, unexplained disability into something anticipated and survivable, and it is the most therapeutic thing in this module.

Comorbidities & drugs to avoid

⚖️

How each comorbidity changes the disease AND the management

ComorbidityHow it changes the COURSEHow it changes the MANAGEMENT
Heart failureThe great confounder. Coexists with ARDS constantly, and each worsens the other: ARDS raises RV afterload, and a failing RV worsens gas exchange. Makes the diagnosis harder and the fluid strategy treacherous.Get an echo — the Berlin criterion effectively requires it. Do not withhold the ARDS diagnosis just because the EF is low. Treat both: lung-protective ventilation plus genuine decongestion. High PEEP helps the failing LV (it reduces preload and afterload) but hurts the failing RV.
COPDBaseline CO₂ retention means the "normal" gas is abnormal. Dynamic hyperinflation and auto-PEEP develop fast and cause hypotension and dyssynchrony.NIV is first-line for the acute hypercapnic exacerbation (strong evidence — reduces intubation and mortality). Target SpO₂ 88–92%, not 94–98%. If ventilated: long expiratory time, lower RR, watch for auto-PEEP (a flow tracing that never returns to zero). Permissive hypercapnia is more dangerous here — they start from a higher CO₂.
Chronic kidney disease / AKIAKI complicates 40–50% of ARDS and independently predicts death. Impairs the compensation for respiratory acidosis and the clearance of most sedatives.Conservative fluids become harder — you have less renal reserve to fix an overshoot. Dose-adjust everything. Avoid vecuronium (active renally-cleared metabolite → prolonged paralysis); use cisatracurium, which is eliminated by organ-independent Hofmann degradation. Early RRT for volume control is often the way to achieve the negative balance.
ObesityReduced FRC and chest-wall compliance; higher pleural pressure. Desaturates alarmingly fast on induction (tiny oxygen reserve). Higher ARDS risk — but, paradoxically, lower mortality once they have it (the "obesity paradox").The single most important error in all of ARDS is dosing tidal volume on actual body weight. Lungs do not grow with weight. Always use predicted body weight, from HEIGHT and SEX only. Obese patients need higher PEEP (to counteract the chest-wall load — the transpulmonary pressure is far lower than the airway pressure suggests; oesophageal manometry helps), reverse Trendelenburg / ramped positioning, and pre-oxygenation before any airway attempt.
Immunocompromise (transplant, chemotherapy, HIV, biologics)Broadens the pathogen list enormously (PJP, CMV, invasive fungal, Nocardia) and worsens outcome. NIV/HFNC failure rates are higher.Bronchoscopy with BAL has a much higher yield here — pursue it early. Add empiric coverage guided by the specific immune defect. Consider PJP prophylaxis and CMV PCR. Have the goals-of-care conversation early; outcomes with ARDS on a background of a haematological malignancy are sobering.
CirrhosisMarkedly worse mortality. Baseline hypoxemia may be hepatopulmonary syndrome (an intrapulmonary vascular dilatation that is itself a shunt — with the classic platypnoea–orthodeoxia: worse sitting up). Ascites splints the diaphragm.Coagulopathy constrains procedures and anticoagulation. Large-volume paracentesis genuinely improves respiratory mechanics — do it. Avoid hepatically-cleared sedatives; cisatracurium is again the right paralytic.
PregnancyReduced FRC + increased oxygen consumption = desaturates extremely fast. Baseline is a compensated respiratory alkalosis (normal PaCO₂ ~30), so a PaCO₂ of 40 in a pregnant patient means impending failure.Target a higher SpO₂ (≥ 95%) — fetal oxygen delivery sits on the steep part of the curve. Neuromuscular diseaseThe pump fails while the lung is normal — so the saturation stays fine until the moment they arrest. Oximetry is dangerously falsely reassuring.Do not monitor these patients with a pulse oximeter — monitor with serial FVC and negative inspiratory force. Avoid succinylcholine (hyperkalaemic arrest from receptor upregulation).
DRUGS TO AVOID / USE WITH CAUTION IN ACUTE RESPIRATORY FAILURE
  • High-flow oxygen in a CO₂-retaining COPD patient — the classic iatrogenic disaster. Raising PaO₂ worsens hypercapnia by releasing hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (which worsens V̇/Q̇ matching and dead space) and by the Haldane effect; blunting of hypoxic respiratory drive is the least important mechanism, despite being the one everyone quotes. Target SpO₂ 88–92%. But note the corollary — never withhold oxygen from a hypoxaemic patient out of fear of this. Hypoxia kills in minutes; hypercapnia takes hours and is treatable with BiPAP.
  • Sedatives / opioids / benzodiazepines in an unmonitored patient with borderline ventilation — you will convert a compensating patient into an apnoeic one. If you must, monitor with capnography.
  • Succinylcholine — avoid in neuromuscular disease, burns > 24–48 h old, crush injury, prolonged immobility, spinal cord injury and pre-existing hyperkalaemia (hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest from upregulated extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors). Use rocuronium instead.
  • Vecuronium and rocuronium (for prolonged infusion) in renal or hepatic failure — accumulation and prolonged paralysis. Use cisatracurium (Hofmann elimination — organ-independent).
  • Etomidate in septic shock — causes dose-dependent adrenal suppression even after a single induction dose. Reasonable alternatives: ketamine (haemodynamically favourable) or a reduced-dose propofol with a pressor ready.
  • Beta-blockers in acute severe bronchospasm — non-selective ones especially. (In stable COPD, cardioselective beta-blockers are safe and should not be withheld when cardiac indications exist.)
  • Aggressive fluid boluses once the patient is out of shock — every litre widens the alveolar leak. See FACTT.
  • Routine systemic corticosteroids for ALL ARDS — the benefit is real in COVID-ARDS and in early moderate–severe ARDS, but is not established for every phenotype, and steroids started late (after ~14 days) were associated with increased mortality in the ARDSNet LaSRS trial. Timing matters.
  • NSAIDs — no role, and they impair the renal compensation you are relying on.
  • High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) — OSCILLATE was stopped early for HARM (in-hospital mortality 47% vs 35%). This is not a rescue therapy; it is a discredited one.
  • Routine recruitment manoeuvres with aggressive PEEP titration — the ART trial (JAMA 2017) found increased 28-day mortality (55.3% vs 49.3%), more barotrauma and more hypotension. Both the ATS and ESICM guidelines recommend against prolonged/aggressive recruitment manoeuvres.
Prevent

USPSTF A/B & prevention

🛡️

Grade A & B recommendations that actually apply here

There is no USPSTF recommendation for "ARDS" — you cannot screen for a syndrome that appears over 48 hours. But ARDS is almost always the end of a chain that starts with a preventable event: a pneumonia, an aspiration, a sepsis, a trauma. These are the Grade A and B recommendations that break that chain, plus the immunisations that prevent the commonest precipitant of all.

ATobacco smoking cessation — ask all adults about tobacco use, advise them to stop, and provide both behavioural interventions and FDA-approved pharmacotherapyAll non-pregnant adultsThe dominant modifiable risk factor for COPD, for pneumonia, and for chronic respiratory failure. Also independently increases ARDS risk (notably after trauma and transfusion).
ATobacco cessation — behavioural interventions (pharmacotherapy not recommended, as evidence is insufficient in this group)Pregnant adultsAs above, plus fetal benefit.

BObesity — offer or refer to intensive, multicomponent behavioural interventionsAdults with BMI ≥ 30Drives obesity-hypoventilation syndrome and OSA; complicates every aspect of ventilation.

These are ACIP/CDC recommendations rather than USPSTF (the USPSTF does not issue vaccine recommendations), but they matter more here than anything above: pneumonia and influenza are the leading direct causes of ARDS.

Escalate

When to send them to the ED

🚑

Red flags — do not manage these in clinic

Call an ambulance — do not send them home, and do not send them to the ED in their own car:
  • SpO₂ < 90% on room air (or < 88% in known chronic hypercapnic COPD), or any new oxygen requirement.
  • Respiratory rate ≥ 30/min — or, more ominously, < 8/min.
  • Cannot speak in full sentences.
  • Accessory-muscle use, tracheal tug, or paradoxical abdominal breathing.
  • Altered mental status — new confusion, agitation, or somnolence.
  • Cyanosis.
  • Haemodynamic instability — SBP < 90, or a lactate you have any way of knowing is up.
  • Silent chest in an asthmatic (no wheeze = no air moving = peri-arrest).
  • A rising or "normalising" PaCO₂ in a tachypnoeic patient — the pump is failing.
  • Any hypoxaemia with haemoptysis (think DAH, PE) or with a clear chest film (think PE).
The two-question clinic triage. (1) "What is the respiratory rate — counted, by me, for 60 seconds?" and (2) "What does the saturation do when they walk?" Those two numbers will correctly triage the overwhelming majority of dyspnoeic clinic patients, and both are free. A resting saturation taken while the patient sits quietly in a chair is the least sensitive test in respiratory medicine.
