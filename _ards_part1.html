Acute Respiratory Failure & ARDS

On this page
Acute respiratory failure is the lung failing at one of its two jobs — getting oxygen in (hypoxemic, PaO₂ < 60) or getting CO₂ out (hypercapnic, PaCO₂ > 45 with acidemia). ARDS is the most severe hypoxemic form: within a week of an insult, the alveolar–capillary barrier leaks, both lungs flood with protein-rich fluid, and the resulting shunt makes the hypoxemia refractory to oxygen. You cannot oxygenate your way out of it — you have to re-open the lung.

Pathophysiology

🧬

Mechanism — the story, from insult to refractory hypoxemia

Start with the only thing the lung is for: bringing gas and blood close enough together that oxygen can diffuse across. The gas sits in ~480 million alveoli. The blood runs through capillaries pressed against them, separated by a barrier so thin — about 0.3 µm — that it is essentially two cell layers back-to-back: an alveolar epithelium (flat type I pneumocytes covering 95% of the surface, plus cuboidal type II cells that make surfactant and act as the progenitor cells) and a capillary endothelium. That barrier is simultaneously the reason gas exchange works and the reason ARDS is catastrophic: it is far too thin to tolerate injury.

The insult. Something injures that barrier — either directly, from the airspace side (pneumonia, aspiration of gastric acid, inhalation injury, near-drowning, pulmonary contusion), or indirectly, from the bloodstream side (sepsis — the single most common cause, pancreatitis, major trauma, massive transfusion/TRALI, burns). The distinction matters clinically: direct ARDS tends to produce patchy, consolidated, poorly recruitable lung; indirect ARDS tends to produce diffuse, more recruitable oedema. Either way, the endpoint converges.

The breakdown. Injured endothelium and dying type I pneumocytes lose their tight junctions, and the barrier becomes permeable. Neutrophils, marginated in the pulmonary capillaries in enormous numbers, are activated and migrate across it, releasing proteases, reactive oxygen species and cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α). This is the crucial point that separates ARDS from every other kind of pulmonary oedema: the fluid that fills the alveoli is not filtered out by high pressure — it leaks out through holes. It is therefore protein-rich (an exudate), not protein-poor (a transudate). It cannot be diuresed away the way cardiogenic oedema can, because the driving force was never hydrostatic pressure.

The flooding, and the collapse. Two things now happen at once. The alveoli fill with proteinaceous fluid, cellular debris and fibrin, which polymerises against the alveolar wall into the eosinophilic hyaline membranes that define diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) on histology. And the type II cells that make surfactant are killed, while the plasma proteins that have leaked into the airspace directly inactivate whatever surfactant is left. Without surfactant, alveolar surface tension rises, and by Laplace's law the small alveoli collapse into the larger ones. So the alveolus is now flooded and collapsed.

The consequence — and the whole reason ARDS behaves the way it does. A flooded, collapsed alveolus is still perfused — the capillary next to it is still carrying blood. But it is no longer ventilated. Blood therefore runs past it, picks up no oxygen at all, and rejoins the pulmonary vein still venous. That is the definition of a right-to-left intrapulmonary shunt, and shunt is the reason ARDS hypoxemia is refractory: the shunted blood never meets gas, so it does not matter what is in the gas. You can turn the FiO₂ to 100% and the shunted fraction is completely unaffected. (The isoshunt graph below makes this concrete: at a 30% shunt, 100% oxygen buys a PaO₂ of only ~100 mmHg; at 50%, only ~49.)

Two more consequences follow. First, the lung that is left — the aerated compartment — is tiny. Gattinoni's CT work showed the aerated volume in ARDS is often the size of a 5-year-old child's lung: the "baby lung." The chest is normal-sized; the functioning lung is not. Second, microvascular thrombosis and capillary obliteration create the opposite lesion — alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused — i.e. dead space. A high dead-space fraction (VD/VT) is one of the strongest independent predictors of death in ARDS, and it is why the PaCO₂ starts climbing even as you ventilate harder.

A flooded city. Think of the lung as a city of 480 million houses, each with a road (airway) and a water pipe (capillary) running past it. Normally, deliveries (oxygen) go from the road into every house. In ARDS a flood fills a third of the houses to the roof. The pipes still run past those flooded houses — the water keeps flowing — but no delivery can get in. Now: sending more delivery trucks down the road (raising the FiO₂) does nothing at all for the flooded houses; the trucks cannot get through a flooded door. The only things that help are pumping the water out and propping the doors open (PEEP), tilting the city so the flooded district drains and the dry district takes the load (prone positioning), and — critically — not sending so much traffic down the few dry streets that you destroy them too (low tidal volume). That last one is the whole idea of ventilator-induced lung injury: the ventilator, set "normally," will finish off the healthy lung that ARDS left you.

Why raising the FiO₂ stops working — shunt vs V̇/Q̇ mismatch

This is the single most important physiological idea in the guide, and it is the one that most often gets skipped. Hypoxemia has five classical mechanisms (low inspired PO₂, hypoventilation, diffusion limitation, V̇/Q̇ mismatch, and shunt), but at the bedside in acute respiratory failure only the last two really compete — and they respond to oxygen in completely opposite ways.

__SVG_UNITS__ __SVG_ISOSHUNT__
The non-rebreather test. Put a hypoxemic patient on a non-rebreather at 15 L/min. If the saturation climbs nicely, the problem is V̇/Q̇ mismatch — think COPD, asthma, PE, atelectasis. If the saturation barely moves, you are looking at shunt, and the differential collapses to a short list: ARDS, dense lobar pneumonia, cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, or an anatomic shunt (intracardiac, AVM). That single manoeuvre reframes the entire admission — and it is free.

The baby lung — why the ventilator becomes the second disease

__SVG_BABYLUNG__
Ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) is not a complication of ARDS — it is a second, iatrogenic ARDS layered on top of the first. Four mechanisms: volutrauma (over-distension of the baby lung by a "normal" tidal volume), barotrauma (frank rupture — pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum), atelectrauma (the shear injury of alveoli repeatedly snapping open on inspiration and slamming shut on expiration — this is what PEEP prevents), and biotrauma (the cytokines released by all of the above spilling into the circulation and driving the multi-organ failure that actually kills most ARDS patients). Note the elegance of the two core vent settings: low tidal volume prevents volutrauma; adequate PEEP prevents atelectrauma. They are treating two different injuries, and you need both.

A "normal" PaCO₂ in a patient who is visibly struggling is a pre-arrest sign. A tachypnoeic patient with a respiratory rate of 35 should have blown their CO₂ down to 25–30. If their PaCO₂ is 40, they are not "fine" — they are tiring, and their minute ventilation is falling as their diaphragm fails. A CO₂ that is rising in an asthmatic or in ARDS means the patient is about to stop breathing. Do not be reassured by a normal number; be alarmed by it in the wrong context.

Step 2 — the A–a gradient: where in the chain is the problem?

The alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient separates "the lung is broken" from "the lung is fine, but nobody is breathing." It is the difference between the oxygen tension you calculate should be in the alveolus and the oxygen tension you actually measure in the artery.

The equations

Alveolar gas equation: PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (Patm − PH₂O) − PaCO₂ / R   =   FiO₂ × (760 − 47) − PaCO₂ / 0.8   at sea level.

A–a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂.   Expected (normal) A–a ≈ (Age / 4) + 4   on room air.

Step 3 — is it ARDS? Berlin (2012) and the new Global Definition (2024)

ARDS is a syndrome, not a disease — there is no confirmatory test, only a set of criteria you must actively apply. This is exactly why it is missed: LUNG SAFE (Bellani, JAMA 2016) found that clinicians recognised ARDS in only ~60% of the patients who met the criteria — and in only 51% of mild cases. The commonest error in ARDS is not treating it badly; it is not calling it.

The new Global Definition of ARDS (2024)

Berlin had three practical problems: it required an arterial blood gas (unavailable in much of the world), it required invasive ventilation or CPAP (so a patient on HFNC could not be called ARDS at all), and it required CXR or CT (unavailable in many settings). The 2024 Global Definition (Matthay et al., AJRCCM 209:37–47) fixes all three. It expands who counts as having ARDS — it does not replace the Berlin severity strata for intubated patients.

SpO₂/FiO₂ is only valid when SpO₂ ≤ 97%. Above that you are on the flat top of the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve, where a saturation of 98% could correspond to a PaO₂ of 100 or of 400 — the ratio becomes meaningless. If your patient is satting 99%, turn the FiO₂ down until the SpO₂ is ≤ 97%, then calculate. The Global Definition also accepts lung ultrasound (bilateral B-lines and/or consolidations) in place of CXR/CT for the imaging criterion, when performed by a trained operator.

Pneumonia — bacterial, viral (influenza, COVID-19), fungal. The single commonest direct cause.

Acute pancreatitis.
Major trauma, multiple fractures, burns.

If there is no risk factor, it is probably not ARDS — go looking for the mimic. The Berlin definition presumes a precipitant. When you cannot name one, the diagnosis you are about to miss is on this list, and several of these are steroid-responsive and rapidly fatal if missed:
  • Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema — the great mimic. Echo, BNP, and the response to diuresis sort it out (see the imaging table below).
  • Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage — vasculitis, anti-GBM, SLE. Clue: dropping haemoglobin with bilateral infiltrates; haemoptysis in only ~⅓. Serially bloodier BAL aliquots are diagnostic. Needs pulse steroids ± plasma exchange, not just a ventilator.
  • Acute eosinophilic pneumonia — often a young new smoker or vaper. BAL eosinophils > 25%. Dramatically steroid-responsive — this is a diagnosis you can actually cure in 48 hours.
  • Acute interstitial pneumonia (Hamman–Rich) — idiopathic ARDS; histologically identical DAD, but with no precipitant.
  • Cryptogenic organising pneumonia (COP), acute exacerbation of IPF, drug-induced lung injury (amiodarone, bleomycin, checkpoint inhibitors), leukaemic infiltration / leukostasis.
Practical rule: if the patient has bilateral infiltrates and no obvious sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, pancreatitis or trauma — get an echo, send a vasculitis screen, check the haemoglobin trend, and think hard about bronchoscopy with BAL before you settle for "ARDS."
