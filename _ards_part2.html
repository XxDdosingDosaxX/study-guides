Signs & symptoms — frequency, and the mechanism behind each

Frequencies below are approximate, pooled from ARDS and acute-hypoxemic-respiratory-failure cohorts (LUNG SAFE and the ARDSNet trial populations). They are teaching anchors, not precise epidemiology — the point is to know which findings are near-universal (and therefore useless for ruling ARDS in) and which are rare (and therefore highly informative when present).

Finding~FrequencyWhy it happens (pathophysiology)
Dyspnoea~95–100%Driven far more by increased work of breathing and by chemoreceptor/J-receptor stimulation than by the hypoxemia itself. The stiff, flooded lung requires a much larger pressure swing for each breath; the brain reads that mismatch between effort and result as air hunger.
Tachypnoea (RR often 30–40+)~90–95%The compensatory response to rising dead space and hypoxemia. Because tidal volume is limited by the stiff lung, the only way to raise minute ventilation is to raise the rate — so the breathing becomes rapid and shallow. RR is the most sensitive and most ignored vital sign in medicine; it rises before the saturation falls.
Hypoxemia refractory to supplemental O₂~90%Shunt. The defining feature. Blood traverses flooded, unventilated alveoli and returns to the left heart still desaturated, so it never encounters the oxygen you are delivering. See the isoshunt curves above.
Bilateral crackles~80%The sound of collapsed small airways and alveoli popping open during inspiration against surfactant-depleted, fluid-lined walls. Often described as fine and "Velcro-like." Absent crackles do not exclude ARDS — consolidation can be silent.
Tachycardia~80%Sympathetic response to hypoxemia, hypercapnia, the work of breathing, and — usually — the underlying sepsis or hypovolaemia driving it all.
Accessory-muscle use / nasal flaring / tracheal tug~60–70%The diaphragm alone can no longer generate the transpulmonary pressure needed to inflate a lung with a compliance of 20–30 mL/cmH₂O, so the sternocleidomastoids, scalenes and intercostals are recruited. This is a physical sign of a failing pump and it precedes decompensation. Look for it before you look at the monitor.
Fever~50–60%Reflects the precipitant (sepsis, pneumonia, pancreatitis) far more than the ARDS. Its absence does not exclude ARDS; its presence tells you where to look for the cause.
Cough (usually non-productive, or frothy)~50%Airway irritant-receptor stimulation by oedema fluid. Frothy pink sputum classically suggests cardiogenic oedema, but can occur in either.
Cyanosis~30–40%Requires ≥ ~5 g/dL of deoxygenated haemoglobin in the capillaries. The trap: an anaemic patient cannot go blue. A patient with Hb 7 can be profoundly hypoxemic and look pink. Conversely a polycythaemic patient looks blue easily. Cyanosis is a measure of absolute deoxyHb, not of oxygen delivery.
Altered mental status (agitation → confusion → obtundation)~30%Early agitation is a hypoxic brain; late somnolence is CO₂ narcosis from a failing pump. The direction of travel is what matters. A patient who was fighting you and is now peaceful has usually not improved — they are about to arrest. This is the most dangerous "improvement" in medicine.
Hypotension / shock~30%Usually the precipitant (septic shock), not the ARDS. But also: high PEEP and hyperinflation reduce venous return; and severe ARDS causes acute cor pulmonale — the RV fails against a hypoxic, hypercapnic, high-PEEP pulmonary vasculature.
Paradoxical abdominal motion (abdomen moves in on inspiration)~10–15%Diaphragmatic fatigue. The exhausted diaphragm is being pulled cephalad by the negative pleural pressure that the accessory muscles are generating, instead of descending. This is a peri-intubation sign. If you see it, prepare the airway now — not after the next gas.
Haemoptysis< 10%Uncommon in ARDS itself. When present, actively pursue an alternative diagnosis — diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, PE with infarction, or an invasive/necrotising infection.
Chest pain< 10%Not a feature of ARDS (the alveoli have no pain fibres). If present, think PE, pneumothorax, ACS, or pleuritis — i.e. a different or additional problem.
The four physical signs that mean "intubate soon," in the order they appear: (1) accessory-muscle use; (2) inability to speak in full sentences; (3) paradoxical abdominal motion (diaphragmatic fatigue); (4) a falling respiratory rate or rising PaCO₂ in a patient who has not improved — the terminal sign, because it means the pump has quit. None of these are on the monitor. All of them are free.

Imaging — what to order, when, and what the finding means

StudyWhen to order itThe finding — and why it appears
Chest radiograph (portable AP)Immediately, in everyone. Repeat daily while intubated, and always after intubation, central line, or any acute deterioration.Bilateral, diffuse, often peripheral alveolar opacities, classically sparing the costophrenic angles, with air bronchograms (an air-filled bronchus silhouetted by fluid-filled alveoli around it). Appears because alveoli that were black (air) become white (protein-rich fluid). Radiographic changes lag the physiology by 12–24 h in both directions — a "clear" film early does not exclude ARDS, and a still-white film on day 5 does not mean you are failing.
CT chestNot required for diagnosis. Order when the CXR is ambiguous, when you suspect a complication (abscess, empyema, pneumothorax, barotrauma), when you suspect a mimic (ILD, malignancy), or with CT-PA if PE is in play.The classic ARDS CT is a gravitational gradient: normally aerated ventral lung on top, ground-glass in the middle, and dense dependent consolidation in the dorsal lung underneath. This is a picture of the lung being crushed by its own oedematous weight — and it is the anatomical argument for prone positioning, which is why the CT is such a powerful teaching image even though you rarely need it to make the call.
Lung ultrasound (POCUS)At the bedside, immediately — faster than a portable film and now formally accepted for the imaging criterion by the 2024 Global Definition.Bilateral B-lines (vertical, laser-like reverberation artefacts arising from the pleural line, ≥3 per field), subpleural consolidations, and a thickened, irregular, fragmented pleural line. B-lines appear because the ultrasound beam bounces between fluid-thickened interlobular septa. The discriminator from cardiogenic oedema: ARDS gives a patchy, heterogeneous distribution with spared areas and an irregular pleura; cardiogenic oedema gives homogeneous, symmetric, gravity-dependent B-lines with a smooth pleural line and usually effusions.
EchocardiographyOrder in everyone where the origin of oedema is not obvious — this is effectively mandated by the Berlin criterion. Urgently if shocked.Answers three questions at once: (1) Is this cardiogenic? (LV function, E/e′, mitral disease). (2) Is the RV failing? — acute cor pulmonale complicates ~20–25% of moderate–severe ARDS and changes management (it argues against high PEEP and permissive hypercapnia, and argues for proning). (3) Is there a patent foramen ovale? — right-to-left shunting through a PFO opens up under high PEEP and can cause hypoxemia wildly out of proportion to the lung.
CT pulmonary angiogramWhen hypoxemia is out of proportion to the infiltrates, when there is a clear lung with severe hypoxemia, or with unexplained RV strain/haemodynamic collapse.ARDS is profoundly prothrombotic, and PE both mimics and complicates it. Remember: a normal CXR with severe hypoxemia is PE until proven otherwise, not ARDS.
ARDS. Bilateral, diffuse alveolar opacities involving all zones — the "white-out." The heart size is normal and there is no vascular redistribution or Kerley B pattern: this is a permeability oedema, not a pressure one.

Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema — the mimic, for contrast. Note what is different: cardiomegaly, upper-lobe vascular redistribution, a more central/perihilar ("bat-wing") distribution, septal (Kerley B) lines, and pleural effusions. These are the radiographic fingerprints of raised hydrostatic pressure — and their absence is what points you to ARDS.
__CREDIT_cxr_cardiac__
Lung ultrasound — "wet lung." The vertical, laser-like B-lines arising from the pleural line and extending to the bottom of the screen are reverberation artefacts from fluid-thickened interlobular septa. Three or more per field is pathological. The 2024 Global Definition now accepts bilateral B-lines/consolidations as satisfying the imaging criterion — which means ARDS can now be diagnosed at the bedside in under two minutes, without radiology.

They coexist far more often than the textbooks admit. A septic patient with an EF of 35% who has been given 6 L of fluid has both. The Berlin criterion is deliberately worded as "not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload" precisely so that you are not forced to choose. Treat both: lung-protective ventilation and a genuinely negative fluid balance.

The labs — why each number moves, and what falsely moves it

TestWhat it does in ARDS / ARFMechanism — and the confounders that will fool you
PaO₂↓↓ — often profoundly, and disproportionately to the FiO₂ deliveredShunt. Confounders: a venous sample masquerading as arterial (check for a pulsatile draw and a plausible PaCO₂/pH); an air bubble in the syringe falsely raises PaO₂ toward 150; a delay in analysis with leucocytosis falsely lowers it (leukocyte larceny — white cells keep consuming O₂ in the syringe). Always interpret PaO₂ against the FiO₂, never alone.
PaCO₂↓ early (compensatory hyperventilation), then ↑ late (rising dead space + fatigue)The trajectory is the whole message. Early, the patient hyperventilates and blows off CO₂. Late, dead space rises (capillary thrombosis) and the diaphragm tires, so CO₂ climbs. A "normalising" CO₂ in a still-tachypnoeic patient is a pre-arrest finding, not a recovery.
pH / bicarbonateRespiratory alkalosis early → mixed respiratory + metabolic acidosis lateThe metabolic component is usually lactate from the underlying shock. On the ventilator, permissive hypercapnia deliberately drives a respiratory acidosis — accept pH ≥ 7.20 (ARDSNet targets 7.30–7.45 and treats below 7.30 by raising RR to a max of 35). Confounder: a patient with chronic CO₂ retention will have a high bicarbonate at baseline — do not chase their "abnormal" gas to a normal number.
A–a gradient↑↑ WidenedSee the equations above. Confounder: the gradient widens normally with age (≈ Age/4 + 4) and with a higher FiO₂ — so a "wide" gradient on 100% oxygen is much less impressive than the same number on room air.
BNP / NT-proBNPUsually low-to-normal in pure ARDS; high in cardiogenic oedemaReleased by ventricular stretch. Confounders are the point here: BNP rises falsely with age, female sex, renal failure (reduced clearance — a big one in the ICU), AF, sepsis itself, and RV strain from PE or the ARDS-induced cor pulmonale. It falls falsely in obesity (a critical, commonly-missed pitfall) and with sacubitril/valsartan (which raises BNP but not NT-proBNP). Use it to rule cardiogenic oedema OUT (BNP < 100), not to rule it in.
Lactate↑ when there is shock or severe tissue hypoxiaAnaerobic metabolism — but also β₂-agonist infusions, epinephrine, liver failure (impaired clearance), thiamine deficiency and metformin. In ARDS a rising lactate almost always reflects the underlying sepsis or a failing RV, not the lung. Trend it; do not treat a single value.
WBC / procalcitonin / culturesDirected at the precipitant, not the ARDSBlood cultures ×2, sputum/tracheal aspirate, urine, and a respiratory viral PCR panel (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV) before antibiotics — but never delay antibiotics for them. Procalcitonin can help stop antibiotics but should not be used to withhold them in a shocked patient.
Creatinine / ureaOften ↑AKI complicates ~40–50% of ARDS and is a major independent predictor of death. Drivers: the underlying sepsis, hypoperfusion, and — importantly — the ventilator itself (high PEEP and high intrathoracic pressure reduce venous return, cardiac output and renal perfusion, and raise renal venous pressure). Your ventilator settings have renal consequences.
TroponinMildly ↑ in manyUsually demand ischaemia (type 2 MI) from hypoxemia, tachycardia and shock — plus RV strain. A modest rise does not mean the patient needs the cath lab; it means they are sick. Interpret with the ECG and echo.
HaemoglobinWatch the trendA falling Hb with new bilateral infiltrates is diffuse alveolar haemorrhage until proven otherwise. Also: transfuse restrictively (threshold Hb 7 g/dL in most critically ill patients) — transfusion is itself a cause of ARDS (TRALI) and of circulatory overload (TACO).
Platelets / coagulationOften ↓Consumptive from sepsis/DIC, and from microvascular thrombosis in the pulmonary circulation. Thrombocytopenia matters practically: it constrains anticoagulation, and it is a relative contraindication to ECMO cannulation.
Dead-space fraction (VD/VT), if you have volumetric capnography↑↑Reflects alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused (capillary obliteration). An elevated dead-space fraction is one of the strongest independent predictors of mortality in ARDS — arguably stronger than the P/F ratio. Its bedside surrogate: a patient who needs a very high minute ventilation to hold a normal CO₂.
