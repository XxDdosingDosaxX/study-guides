Admit

Who goes where — and why the floor is usually wrong

📋

Admission triage

Established cardiogenic shock (SCAI Stage C or worse) does not belong on a medical floor. It belongs in a CICU/ICU, at a PCI-capable centre, with a shock team activated. If you are admitting a Stage C patient to the floor because "the unit is full," you have made a decision with a mortality attached to it — escalate to the intensivist and the bed manager rather than accepting the bed. The honest role of the ward in this disease is (1) the Stage A/B patient at risk, and (2) the patient who has recovered and come back down.

Two ward patients deserve special suspicion. (1) The "ADHF" admission who isn't responding to diuresis. If their creatinine is climbing and their pressure is drifting down as you diurese, they are not diuretic-resistant — they are cold, and you are unloading a patient who needed output first. Reassess the profile before you double the Lasix. (2) The post-MI patient on day 2–7 who suddenly deteriorates with a new murmur. That is a mechanical complication until an echo says otherwise, and it is a surgical emergency, not a "worsening heart failure" problem.

Treat

Acute management — perfusion before decongestion

💊

The stepwise approach on the floor

the sequencing rule from the pathophysiology section is the whole of ward management: (1) perfusion pressure → (2) flow → (3) fix the cause → (4) decongest → (5) wean. Almost every ward mistake in this disease is doing step 4 before steps 1–2.
1
Recognise, stage, and call for help
The first 10 minutes decide the next 10 days
▾
  • Stage them (SCAI A–E) and say the stage out loud in your escalation call. It is a shared language for exactly this moment.
  • ECG, lactate, echo — the 2025 ACC guidance wants all three as soon as CS is suspected. The echo is the one that changes what you do next.
  • Activate the shock team if your hospital has one. A standardised team-based approach is associated with improved outcomes and is the central recommendation of the 2025 ACC guidance. If you don't have one, call the intensivist and the interventional cardiologist yourself — in parallel, not in sequence.
  • Find the precipitant. Ischaemia, arrhythmia, mechanical complication, infection, non-adherence, NSAIDs, a new negative inotrope, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, PE. Some of these are fixed in minutes.
Before any drug: is this actually cardiogenic? Tamponade, massive PE, RV infarct and acute valve rupture all present as "shock with a high JVP" and each has a specific fix that no vasopressor substitutes for. This is why the echo comes first.
2
Fluid — a small, deliberate challenge, or none at all
The most misused intervention in this disease
▾

The reflex "hypotension → bolus" is wrong by default here, because PCWP >15 is part of the definition — the tank is already full. Look again at the function curve: on a flat Starling relationship, added preload buys almost no stroke volume and plenty of alveolar oedema.

ProfileFluid?Why
Cold & wet (JVD, rales, oedema, cool)NoAlready congested. Fluid worsens Loop 2 and buys nothing. They need output, then decongestion.
Cold & dry (hypoperfused, flat JVP, clear lungs)A cautious 250 mL challenge, then reassessThe one CS profile where preload may genuinely be low. Reassess immediately — if there's no response, stop and move to inotropy. Do not repeat a bolus that didn't work.
RV infarct / RV-predominant (high JVP, clear lungs, inferior STEMI, PAPi <0.9)Yes — this is the exceptionManagement inverts. The stiff, infarcted RV needs preload to push blood across to a starved LV. Give fluid, avoid nitrates and diuretics entirely, and preserve AV synchrony (pace AV-sequentially if you must). Diuresing an RV infarct is a classic, lethal error.
3
Restore perfusion pressure — norepinephrine
Buys the myocardium back its own blood supply
▾

If MAP is <65 with hypoperfusion, start norepinephrine — and start it through a good peripheral line while central access is being obtained rather than waiting. Full dosing, receptor pharmacology and the alternatives are in the ICU → Pressors & inotropes table; on the floor the essential points are:

  • Norepinephrine is first-line (start ~0.05 mcg/kg/min; titrate q2–5 min to MAP ≥65).
  • Not dopamine. SOAP II found roughly double the arrhythmia rate (24.1% vs 12.4%, p<0.001) and a mortality signal in the prespecified cardiogenic shock subgroup.
  • Not phenylephrine — pure α₁ with no inotropy raises afterload against a failing ventricle and can drop cardiac output outright.
  • A patient on norepinephrine needs an arterial line and an ICU — which is the point: starting this drug is the escalation decision.
this looks paradoxical — why constrict a ventricle that's already struggling against afterload? Because of Loop 1. Coronary perfusion is driven by aortic diastolic pressure minus LVEDP. Restore that gradient and the ischaemic myocardium gets blood; leave it collapsed and nothing else you do can work. You accept an afterload cost to stop the heart from starving itself.
4
Restore flow — add an inotrope
Once the pressure is adequate but the organs still aren't perfused
▾

If the MAP is ≥65 but the lactate is still rising, urine still low and extremities still cool, the problem is flow, not pressure — add dobutamine or milrinone. DOREMI found no difference between them in the primary composite (49% vs 54%, p=0.47), so choose on physiology: milrinone if they're β-blocked or have pulmonary hypertension; dobutamine if renal function is poor or the blood pressure is marginal. Full dose ladders in the ICU panel.

Adding a second vasoactive agent moves the patient to SCAI Stage D by definition — and Stage D is SCAI's explicit trigger to transfer to a shock hub. If you find yourself reaching for drug number two on a medical floor, the real decision isn't which drug. It's that this patient needed to leave your floor some time ago.
5
Decongest — last, not first
Only once they're warm and perfusing
▾

Once perfusion is restored, offload the congestion that's drowning the lungs and choking the kidneys.

Fix the output first; the kidneys will follow. This exact error — escalating diuretics in a patient who needed inotropy — is one of the most common ways a Stage B/C patient is converted into a Stage D one on a medical floor.

Context

How comorbidities change this disease

🔗

The company cardiogenic shock keeps

ComorbidityHow it changes the courseHow it changes management
CKD / AKIBidirectional and vicious: low output plus venous congestion injures kidneys, and the failing kidney retains salt and water, worsening congestion. Renal dysfunction is embedded in both SCAI Stage C and the IABP-SHOCK II score. AKI independently worsens mortality across every SCAI stage.Avoid milrinone — ~90% renally excreted, half-life 2–2.5 h that prolongs dramatically; the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline notes accumulation in renal failure. Use dobutamine instead, or dose-reduce milrinone (CrCl 30 → ~0.33; CrCl 10 → ~0.23 mcg/kg/min). Minimise contrast; do not withhold life-saving angiography over contrast fear in AMI-CS. Consider early CRRT for refractory congestion.
DiabetesSilent ischaemia (autonomic neuropathy) means the warning chest pain never comes — they present later and sicker. Diffuse multivessel disease makes revascularisation harder. Admission glucose >191 mg/dL scores in IABP-SHOCK II.Hold SGLT2i while acutely ill/NPO (euglycaemic DKA). Insulin infusion rather than orals. Have a lower threshold to suspect ischaemia in an atypical presentation — "no chest pain" means nothing here.
Atrial fibrillationLoses the atrial kick — up to ~20–30% of ventricular filling in a stiff or ischaemic ventricle. In a marginal patient, new AF with RVR alone can precipitate shock. Rapid rates also shorten diastole → less filling and less coronary perfusion.If AF is causing the shock, cardiovert — don't titrate drugs. Rate control with β-blockers or diltiazem is contraindicated in decompensated low output (negative inotropy). Amiodarone is the safest agent here. Anticoagulate.
Chronic HFrEF (HF-CS)The largest group in contemporary registries. They've adapted to low output and low BP, so they under-declare severity — SCAI explicitly warns they may display a falsely reassuring picture despite dire haemodynamics. No artery to open.Judge against their baseline, not population norms. The AMI-CS trial evidence (SHOCK, DanGer) doesn't transfer. The real questions become recovery vs durable LVAD vs transplant vs palliation — decided by advanced HF, ideally before now.
COPDMuddies everything: dyspnoea is attributed to the lungs, β-blockers get withheld, and hypoxia + hypercapnia raise pulmonary vascular resistance → RV strain.Use bisoprolol (β₁-selective) rather than carvedilol once stable. Treat hypoxia and hypercapnia — they're a reversible RV afterload. Beware β₂-agonist nebs driving tachycardia and hypokalaemia in an irritable myocardium.
RV dysfunction / pulmonary hypertensionChanges the physiology completely. The RV is a volume pump that fails against pressure; once it dilates, the septum bows left and reduces LV filling (ventricular interdependence). PAPi <0.9 flags it.Preload-dependent: fluids, not diuresis. Avoid nitrates. Maintain AV synchrony. Avoid hypoxia/hypercapnia/acidosis (all raise PVR). Milrinone is attractive (lowers PVR); consider inhaled pulmonary vasodilators. An LV-only device (Impella CP) in an RV-failure patient can make things worse.
Prior strokeStrongest single predictor in IABP-SHOCK II (2 points). Marks diffuse vascular disease and constrains anticoagulation choices.Weighs heavily against MCS candidacy (all devices need anticoagulation; all bleed). Factor into goals-of-care discussions early.
Advanced age / frailtyOne of the strongest and most consistent mortality modifiers across every SCAI stage. Age >73 scores in IABP-SHOCK II. SCAI considered making it a formal modifier but declined — no clean binary threshold exists.SCAI's framing is the honest one: age functions as a marker of comorbidity, frailty and candidacy for (or futility of) intervention. It should inform the conversation, not be a rule. Transfers for futile care in unrecoverable patients don't change outcomes and consume capacity that another patient needs.
AnaemiaDO₂ = CO × Hb × SaO₂ × 1.34 — with a fixed low CO, haemoglobin is one of the few remaining levers on oxygen delivery.No trial-defined transfusion threshold specific to CS; most use a restrictive strategy (~7–8 g/dL) with a higher threshold in active ischaemia. Correct iron deficiency. If MCS is coming, haemolysis will drop the haemoglobin further — plan ahead.
Infection / sepsisThe commonest precipitant of HF decompensation, and it creates a genuinely mixed shock — the septic vasodilation layers onto Loop 4's inflammatory vasodilation, and the SVR read becomes uninterpretable.Treat both. This is a setting where a PA catheter earns its keep: it tells you whether you're fighting a flow problem or a resistance problem, which completely changes the drug.
Drugs to avoid or use with real caution in cardiogenic shock
DrugWhy it hurts here
β-blockers (acutely)Negative inotropy in a ventricle with no reserve → precipitates or deepens shock. Hold in anyone hypotensive, congested or hypoperfused. Restart only when warm, dry and off pressors — but do restart, because chronically they're a mortality-reducing pillar.
Non-dihydropyridine CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem)Potent negative inotropes. Contraindicated in decompensated HFrEF and in shock. A common trap when someone reaches for rate control in AF with RVR — use amiodarone or cardiovert instead.
DopamineSOAP II: ~2× the arrhythmia rate vs norepinephrine (24.1% vs 12.4%, p<0.001) and a mortality signal in the prespecified cardiogenic shock subgroup. There is no scenario in CS where it is the best available drug.
PhenylephrinePure α₁ with no inotropic support — raises afterload against a failing ventricle and can drop cardiac output outright. Reserve for the narrow case of LVOT obstruction (e.g. HOCM), where you actually want more afterload and a slower heart.
NSAIDsProstaglandin inhibition → Na⁺/water retention, blunted diuretic response, worsened renal perfusion when the kidney is already prostaglandin-dependent. A classic outpatient precipitant.
Thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone)Cause fluid retention and precipitate HF. Contraindicated in NYHA III–IV.
Class I antiarrhythmics & most Class III (flecainide, sotalol, dronedarone)Proarrhythmic and negatively inotropic in structural heart disease (the CAST lesson). Dronedarone increases mortality in decompensated HF. Amiodarone is the antiarrhythmic of choice in this population.
Nitrates & morphine in RV infarctBoth drop preload — and the infarcted RV is entirely preload-dependent. Can cause abrupt, profound hypotension. Also avoid nitrates within 24–48 h of a PDE5 inhibitor.
Over-diuresis in a cold patientNot a drug so much as a reflex, and one of the commonest iatrogenic escalations to Stage D on a medical floor. Fix output before you unload volume.
Excess supplemental O₂Hyperoxia causes coronary vasoconstriction and increased infarct size (AVOID, DETO2X-AMI). Target SpO₂ 92–96%; do not give O₂ to a non-hypoxic patient.
Milrinone in AKI/ESRD~90% renal clearance; accumulates, causing prolonged hypotension you cannot turn off (half-life 2–2.5 h, much longer in renal failure vs dobutamine's ~2 min). Use dobutamine or dose-reduce.

Home

Independent, back to baseline function, reliable, has support and transport. Off inotropes ≥48 h, stable on oral regimen. Follow-up already booked — not "will be called."

Home health

Home-safe but needs skilled nursing for weights/labs/med reconciliation, or IV diuretic bridging. Ideal for the newly optimised, complex GDMT patient who needs eyes on them weekly.

SNF

Needs 24-hour skilled nursing but <3 h therapy/day. Common after a prolonged ICU stay with deconditioning, delirium, or new dialysis needs.

Acute rehab (IRF)

Can tolerate ≥3 h therapy/day across ≥2 disciplines and has rehab potential. Typical after a long ICU course with ICU-acquired weakness in an otherwise recoverable patient.

LTAC

Prolonged complex care expected >25 days — chronic vent weaning, complex wounds, long-term IV access. The post-ECMO/post-arrest patient who survived but didn't recover.

Hospice / palliative

Refractory Stage D HF, not an LVAD/transplant candidate, or inotrope-dependent with goals favouring comfort. Palliative inotropes at home are a legitimate, guideline-recognised option — a symptom therapy, not a failure.
A patient who survives cardiogenic shock has just been given the most reliable prognostic information of their life, and it is a good moment to use it. This is the window for the advanced-HF referral, the ICD/CRT decision, the transplant/LVAD evaluation, and the goals-of-care conversation — while they're awake, lucid, and have seen what the disease can do. The version of this conversation that happens at 3am on two pressors with a surrogate decision-maker is far worse for everyone.
