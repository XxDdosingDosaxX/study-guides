Clinical Reference

Cardiogenic Shock

On this page
A pump so weak it can no longer perfuse the body — sustained hypotension (SBP <90 for >30 min, or vasopressors needed to hold it there) plus end-organ hypoperfusion, caused by cardiac dysfunction rather than volume loss or vasodilation. The compensations that save you in hemorrhage make this one worse.

The downward spiral — why this disease accelerates

Every other shock state has a fix that matches the problem: give blood for hemorrhage, give antibiotics and pressors for sepsis. Cardiogenic shock is different, and harder, for one reason — the body's own compensatory response makes it worse. Understanding that single fact explains essentially every treatment decision below.

Start with the insult. A large anterior MI wipes out a critical mass of left ventricle (classically ~40%); or a fulminant myocarditis stuns the whole myocardium; or a chronically dilated heart finally decompensates. Stroke volume falls, so cardiac output falls. From there, four vicious loops open at once.

Loop 1 — the heart starves itself

Falling output means a falling mean arterial pressure. But remember where coronary perfusion comes from: the left coronary fills in diastole, driven by the gradient between aortic diastolic pressure and LV end-diastolic pressure. Cardiogenic shock attacks both ends of that gradient simultaneously — MAP (and therefore aortic diastolic pressure) falls, while LVEDP rises because the failing ventricle can't empty. The gradient collapses. The ischemic myocardium becomes more ischemic, more muscle stuns, and stroke volume falls further. This is the loop that kills fastest, and it is the reason we give a vasoconstrictor to a heart that is already struggling against afterload — a myocardium with no blood supply cannot recover no matter how good the rest of your plan is.

Loop 2 — the lungs drown

Blood the ventricle can't eject backs up. LVEDP rises into the left atrium and pulmonary veins; PCWP climbs past 15, then past 25, and fluid transudates into the alveoli. Now the patient is hypoxemic — and hypoxemic blood arriving at an already-ischemic myocardium deepens the ischemia, feeding straight back into Loop 1.

Loop 3 — the compensations betray you

The baroreceptors cannot tell the difference between "the pump has failed" and "you are bleeding to death." They read low output the same way, and turn on the same two ancient rescue systems: the sympathetic nervous system (catecholamine surge → tachycardia, vasoconstriction) and RAAS (angiotensin II → vasoconstriction; aldosterone → salt and water retention). In hemorrhage this is brilliant. Here it is a catastrophe:

  • Vasoconstriction raises afterload — the failing ventricle must now eject against a higher wall of resistance, so stroke volume falls further.
  • Tachycardia shortens diastole — which is both when the ventricle fills and when the coronaries perfuse. You lose preload and coronary flow in one move.
  • Salt and water retention adds volume to a ventricle that is already over-filled, worsening Loop 2.
  • All of it raises myocardial oxygen demand while Loop 1 is cutting supply.

Loop 4 — the inflammatory arm (why some shock patients look "warm")

In a substantial minority, prolonged hypoperfusion triggers a systemic inflammatory response: inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) is switched on, flooding the circulation with nitric oxide and peroxynitrite, alongside IL-6 and TNF-α. Nitric oxide is both a potent vasodilator and a direct myocardial depressant. So a patient who began cold and clamped-down can become inappropriately vasodilated — SVR falls, the picture turns "mixed," and pressor requirements escalate for reasons that have nothing to do with the original infarct. This is why a cardiogenic shock patient can look deceptively septic, and why the SHOCK trial registry found many patients had a normal or low SVR.

Left alone the loops converge: AKI, congestive hepatopathy and shock liver, bowel ischemia, and death. Despite four decades of trials, in-hospital mortality remains roughly 30–50% (2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance). Every therapy below exists to break one of these four loops.

a failing bilge pump on a boat that's taking on water. The crew's instinct is to scream "pump harder!" while narrowing the outlet pipe so the water sprays further away — that's your sympathetic surge raising afterload. But the pump is already dying, and now it's straining against a narrower pipe while the water keeps rising. Worse, the rising water shorts out the pump's own power supply — that's coronary perfusion collapsing. Every instinct the crew has accelerates the sinking. Your job is exactly three things: plug the hole (revascularize), take over the pumping or widen the pipe (inotropes, unloading, mechanical support), and keep just enough pressure in the system to keep the pump's own power supply live (norepinephrine to a MAP of 65) — without cranking the resistance so high you finish the pump off.

The hemodynamic definition — what the numbers actually mean

Cardiogenic shock is defined by a combination of a pressure problem, a flow problem, and a perfusion problem. Any one alone is not shock.

CriterionThresholdWhat it's telling you
Sustained hypotensionSBP <90 mmHg for >30 min
or vasopressors/MCS required to keep SBP ≥90		The perfusion pressure driving every organ bed — including the coronaries — has failed. The "or pressors" clause matters: a patient at SBP 105 on norepinephrine is still in shock. Never let a treated number reassure you.
Low cardiac indexCI <2.2 L/min/m²
(<1.8 without support)		The flow problem — this is what makes it cardiogenic. Normal CI is 2.5–4.0. This is the number that separates cardiogenic from septic shock, where CI is typically high.
Elevated filling pressurePCWP >15 mmHgProves the low output is not hypovolemia — the tank is full and the pump still isn't moving it. This is the criterion that stops you reflexively giving fluid.

UOP <30 mL/h · AMS · cool, mottled skin		The consequence — and the thing you actually treat. Per SCAI 2022, lactate >2 mmol/L is consistent with at least Stage C. Hypoperfusion without hypotension carries a higher mortality than hypotension with preserved perfusion.

Hemodynamic criteria follow the SHOCK trial entry definition (CI ≤2.2, PCWP ≥15) and the SCAI SHOCK 2022 Expert Consensus Update.

Normotensive cardiogenic shock is real and it is dangerous. Roughly 5–10% of patients in the SHOCK trial registry had a preserved SBP but a cardiac index in the shock range and clear hypoperfusion. SCAI explicitly notes that in Stage C, "hypotension is not required." If the lactate is up, the urine has stopped, and the extremities are cool — the patient is in shock, whatever the cuff says. Chronic HF patients are the classic trap: they have adapted to a low output and a low baseline BP, so they look falsely reassuring while their hemodynamics are dire.

Why the failing heart can't be fixed with fluid — the function curve

The Frank–Starling relationship says stroke volume rises with filling pressure. On a normal curve that's steep and useful: give fluid, get output. On the cardiogenic shock curve it is nearly flat — you can push the filling pressure from 18 to 30 and gain almost no stroke volume, while guaranteeing pulmonary edema. This one picture explains why "the patient is hypotensive, give a bolus" is the wrong reflex here.

On the flat shock curve, adding preload buys almost no stroke volume but plenty of pulmonary oedema. Only an inotrope (or mechanical support) lifts the curve itself; diuresis and vasodilation move the patient left out of congestion. This is the whole logic of the Stevenson profiles below.

Classification — the three questions you must answer

On rounds you will be asked three separate questions about a shock patient, and they are genuinely independent: what caused it, how sick is it, and what's the hemodynamic profile. Answer all three.

1. What caused it? (etiology drives the fix)

The historical teaching that cardiogenic shock is an MI disease is now out of date. In contemporary cardiac ICU registries — including the Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network — AMI-CS accounts for less than one third of cases; more than two thirds are non-ischaemic, predominantly heart-failure-related, and their outcomes are at least as poor. That matters enormously, because the entire randomised evidence base — SHOCK, IABP-SHOCK II, CULPRIT-SHOCK, DanGer Shock, ECLS-SHOCK — was built in AMI-CS. Every time you apply those trials to your end-stage HFrEF patient, you are extrapolating beyond the data. Do it consciously.

2. How sick is it? (SCAI SHOCK stage drives escalation)

The SCAI SHOCK classification (2019, updated 2022) replaced the old binary "in shock / not in shock" with a five-stage ladder, endorsed by ACC, AHA, ESC-ACVC, SCCM, STS and ISHLT. Its value is less about prognosis than about communication and trajectory — everyone from the paramedic to the transplant surgeon says the same word and means the same thing, and a stage that changes is more informative than a stage that sits still.

The mortality cliff sits between C and D (12.4% → 40.4%). That is exactly why SCAI advises spoke centres to trigger transfer on progression to Stage D — before Stage E. Waiting for extremis is waiting too long.

3. What's the profile? (Stevenson quadrants drive the immediate move)

Two bedside questions — is there congestion (wet or dry) and is there hypoperfusion (warm or cold) — sort every patient into four boxes. Cardiogenic shock lives in the bottom two.

ProfileFindingsHemodynamicsThe move
A — Warm & dryCompensated; no congestion, perfusingPCWP normal, CI normalNot shock. Optimize GDMT and find the precipitant.
B — Warm & wetCongested but perfusing: orthopnea, JVD, rales, warm extremitiesPCWP ↑, CI adequateClassic ADHF, not shock. Diurese ± vasodilate. If you reach for an inotrope here you are causing harm.
C — Cold & wetThe commonest CS profile (~2/3)PCWP ↑ and CI ↓Both problems at once. Restore perfusion first (norepinephrine ± inotrope), then decongest. Diuresing a cold patient before you fix the output just drops their pressure further.
L — Cold & dryHypoperfused without congestion; the "euvolemic-cold" patientPCWP normal/low, CI ↓The one profile where a cautious fluid challenge (250 mL) is reasonable — but reassess immediately. If no response, they need inotropy, not more volume.
Read the patternCongestion signs (JVD, S3, orthopnea, rales, oedema) say wet. Low-output signs (cool extremities, narrow pulse pressure, oliguria, AMS, hyponatraemia, rising lactate) say cold. Cold is what makes it shock; wet only tells you where the volume is. A patient can be cold and dry and still be dying.

Where it starts. An occlusive plaque in a proximal vessel puts a large territory of myocardium at risk. Lose enough contractile mass — classically ~40% of the LV — and the pump fails outright.

The infarct under the microscope, staged. Myofibre waviness → interstitial oedema → hypereosinophilia and coagulative necrosis → neutrophil infiltration. The timeline matters clinically: the mechanical complications cluster when the necrotic wall is at its weakest, days 2–7, as macrophages digest the scaffolding before fibrosis sets in.
__CRED_mi_histo__
Dilated cardiomyopathy — the other road in. No culprit artery to open. The ventricle has remodelled into a thin-walled sphere: poor geometry, high wall stress, low output. When this heart decompensates into shock the question is not "which vessel" but "recover, LVAD, transplant, or palliate."

Why each therapy works — the through-line of this whole guide

Every drug and device below interrupts a named step in the spiral. If you can say which arrow you're cutting, you understand the treatment. If you can't, you're pattern-matching.

TherapyMolecular mechanismWhich loop it cuts
Norepinephrineα₁-adrenergic agonist (≫β₁) → Gq → phospholipase C → IP₃ → vascular smooth-muscle Ca²⁺ → vasoconstrictionLoop 1. Raises aortic diastolic pressure, restoring the DBP−LVEDP gradient that perfuses the coronaries. It doesn't fix the pump — it buys the pump back its own blood supply. This is why we accept the afterload cost.
Dobutamineβ₁-agonist → Gs → adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates L-type Ca²⁺ channels & phospholamban → ↑Ca²⁺ transientLoop 1 (flow arm). Directly raises contractility → stroke volume → cardiac output. Lifts the Starling curve itself rather than sliding along it.
MilrinonePhosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor → blocks cAMP breakdown → same ↑cAMP/PKA endpoint, but downstream of the β-receptorLoop 1 + Loop 3. Works when the β-receptor is blocked (β-blocker on board) or downregulated (chronic HF) — dobutamine can't. Also vasodilates arterial and pulmonary beds ("inodilator"), so it lowers the afterload that Loop 3 raised.
Emergent PCIRestores epicardial flow to the culprit territory (TIMI 3)The insult itself. The only therapy that removes the cause rather than supporting around it — and the only one with a durable mortality benefit in AMI-CS (SHOCK, benefit at 6 months and persisting to 6 years).
Mechanical support (Impella / ECMO)Trans-valvular axial pump or extracorporeal circuit generating forward flow independent of native contractionLoops 1, 2 and 3 at once — the reason MCS is conceptually so attractive. It unloads the LV (↓LVEDP → widens the coronary gradient and cuts wall stress/O₂ demand), provides forward flow (↑MAP, ↑perfusion), and lets you back off catecholamines.
Diuretics / vasodilatorsLoop-of-Henle Na-K-2Cl blockade; NO-donor venodilationLoop 2. Lowers PCWP → clears the alveoli → improves oxygenation. Sequencing rule: perfusion before decongestion. Diuresing a cold patient drops preload on a flat curve and buys you hypotension for nothing.
Revascularization of culprit onlyAvoids contrast load, procedure time and no-reflow in non-culprit territoriesIatrogenic loop. CULPRIT-SHOCK: opening everything at once increased death/renal failure. Restraint is the intervention.
the sequence is always the same and it is worth memorising: (1) perfusion pressure so the heart and brain stay alive → (2) flow so the organs recover → (3) fix the cause → (4) decongest → (5) wean. Doing (4) before (1) is the most common way residents make a shock patient worse.
