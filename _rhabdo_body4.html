🫀 ICU — crush, compartments, dialysis & the hyperthermic syndromes
Admit

ICU triggers & severity

🚨

When rhabdomyolysis belongs in the unit

Rhabdomyolysis severe enough for the ICU is a different disease from the CK of 6,000 on the wards. In a French multicentre ICU cohort of 387 patients with severe rhabdomyolysis (Ann Intensive Care 2020): 81.4% developed AKI, 44.1% reached KDIGO Stage 3, and 26.6% required renal replacement therapy.

And the punchline of that cohort: severe AKI correlated with admission phosphate, potassium, bicarbonate and myoglobin — but not with CK. Once again: the number everyone quotes on rounds is the number that predicts the least. Look at the chemistry panel, not the CK.
Admit to the ICU for:
  • Life-threatening hyperkalemia — K⁺ ≥6.5, any hyperkalemic ECG change beyond peaked T waves, or a potassium that rebounds after treatment because myonecrosis is ongoing.
  • AKI requiring RRT, or oliguria/anuria despite adequate resuscitation.
  • Compartment syndrome, or a limb requiring serial pressure measurement.
  • Crush injury / prolonged entrapment — high-volume resuscitation with an anticipated reperfusion potassium surge.
  • Haemodynamic instability or vasopressor requirement.
  • Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.2).
  • Any hyperthermic rigidity syndrome — malignant hyperthermia, NMS, serotonin syndrome, or exertional heat stroke. These need active cooling, paralysis, and an antidote.
  • Propofol infusion syndrome — unexplained metabolic acidosis and rising lactate on prolonged propofol.
  • DIC or multi-organ failure.
  • Respiratory failure — from volume overload, from the underlying trigger, or because you need to paralyse them to control muscular hyperthermia.

Limb

Compartment syndrome — the one you cannot miss

🔪

Cause, consequence — and iatrogenic complication

Compartment syndrome is simultaneously a cause of rhabdomyolysis, a consequence of it, and a complication of treating it. Injured muscle swells inside an inelastic fascial envelope → pressure exceeds capillary perfusion pressure → the muscle infarcts → it swells more. And your fluid resuscitation feeds the loop — aggressive fluid is a recognised precipitant of non-traumatic compartment syndrome. The only thing that breaks the cycle is a scalpel.
SignTimingNote
Pain out of proportion; pain on passive stretchEarliestThe most sensitive clinical sign. Passive stretch of the muscles in the compartment reproduces severe pain.
Tense, "woody" compartmentEarlyPalpate and compare with the other limb. Document it.
ParaesthesiaIntermediateNerve ischaemia — the compartment's nerve is the first structure to complain.
Pallor, paralysis, pulselessnessLATEBy the time these appear, you have already lost the limb. Never wait for the "6 Ps" — a palpable pulse does not exclude compartment syndrome, because compartment pressure need only exceed capillary pressure, not arterial pressure.
A CK that plateaus or re-risesAny timeThe lab clue — and often the only clue you get in a sedated patient. CK should fall by roughly 39% of the previous day's value. If it isn't falling, muscle is still dying: go and examine the limbs.
Your rhabdomyolysis patient is often the one who cannot tell you. Sedated, intubated, obtunded, intoxicated, post-ictal — that is the population that gets rhabdomyolysis, and it is exactly the population in whom every clinical sign above is unobtainable. In that patient, a low threshold to MEASURE is the whole of the art.

The number that saves the limb

Delta pressure = diastolic BP − intracompartmental pressure. A delta pressure ≤30 mmHg is the accepted threshold for fasciotomy (McQueen & Court-Brown; AAOS acute compartment syndrome CPG). It is more reliable than an absolute pressure — because a hypotensive patient reaches a critical perfusion deficit at a much lower absolute compartment pressure.

The hypotension trap. An absolute compartment pressure of 28 mmHg sounds reassuring — until you notice the diastolic BP is 50. That delta of 22 mmHg means the muscle is being infarcted right now. In a shocked patient, never use the absolute number. Use the delta.
The fasciotomy decision in NON-traumatic rhabdomyolysis is genuinely contested — know both sides. In traumatic compartment syndrome, fasciotomy is unambiguous and urgent. In non-traumatic rhabdomyolysis (found-down, drug-induced), you may be opening muscle that is already necrotic in a patient who is septic or immunosuppressed — creating a large open wound, and some series report fasciotomy may preserve function at the cost of a higher amputation risk. The counterweight, and the prevailing bias, is that an unnecessary fasciotomy is better than a late one. The defensible practice: measure the pressures, involve surgery EARLY, and make the decision jointly. Never let "rhabdo is a medical problem" delay the surgical consult.

Crush

Crush syndrome — the mass-casualty version

🏚️

The ISN field protocol — and "smiling death"

"Smiling death" / rescue death. The entrapped victim is alert and talking while compressed — because the necrotic limb's potassium is trapped in a compartment with no blood flow. The moment the slab is lifted and the limb reperfuses, that potassium hits the circulation, and the patient arrests. This is why the single most important intervention in crush injury happens before extrication.

From the ISN Renal Disaster Relief Task Force recommendations (Sever & Vanholder, Nephrol Dial Transplant 2012) — the definitive crush protocol, distilled from earthquake registries in which >1,200 victims were dialysed:

Bywaters & Beall first described it in victims of the 1941 London Blitz, and it remains the deadliest form of this disease.

Support

Renal replacement therapy

🩺

Dialyse the patient, not the CK

RRT in rhabdomyolysis treats the CONSEQUENCES of kidney failure — it does not treat rhabdomyolysis. The indications are the standard AKI indications and nothing more.
Start RRT for…Threshold
Refractory hyperkalemiaK⁺ that will not stay down despite calcium, insulin/dextrose, albuterol and binders — especially when myonecrosis is ongoing and reloading it. This is the commonest indication in rhabdomyolysis, and it should be anticipated, not reacted to.
Refractory volume overloadPulmonary oedema in an oliguric patient — frequently caused by the resuscitation.
Severe metabolic acidosispH <7.1–7.2 refractory to medical therapy.
Uremic complicationsEncephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding.
Progressive, deteriorating kidney functionClinical judgment; KDIGO Stage 3 with no sign of recovery.
Do NOT dialyse to "clear the myoglobin," and do NOT dialyse prophylactically. DASAIM/DSIT 2019 suggests against routine RRT. Yes — high-cutoff membranes, super-high-flux haemofiltration and CVVHDF do clear myoglobin (reported clearances ~80–90 mL/min, and grams removed per day). And yet mortality is unchanged.

Why it fails, and this is the whole lesson of this disease: myoglobin peaks at 8–12 h and is essentially gone by 24 h. By the time your patient has an established AKI in front of you, the myoglobin has already been filtered and has already done its damage. You would be extracting the arsonist from a building that has already burned down.

The one exception is crush syndrome, where the ISN endorses pre-emptive dialysis in patients at high risk of hyperkalemia — not to remove myoglobin, but because the ongoing potassium load is lethal and relentless.

The hyperthermic rigidity syndromes

🌡️

Three ways to cook a muscle — and three different antidotes

All three produce hyperthermia + rigidity + rhabdomyolysis + autonomic instability, and all three land in the ICU looking similar. Getting the differential right changes the drug you reach for.

The one-question discriminator: check the reflexes and look for clonus. Hyperreflexia + clonus + mydriasis + hyperactive bowel sounds = serotonin syndrome. Lead-pipe rigidity + hyporeflexia + normal pupils = NMS. Clonus is the most robust single feature of serotonin toxicity — the Hunter criteria, built around it, are ~84% sensitive and ~97% specific.

Benzodiazepines Titrate generously to control agitation and rigidity. The workhorse in both NMS and serotonin syndrome. Muscular rigidity is the heat source — sedating the muscle is antipyretic therapy. Respiratory depression; be ready to intubate.
In severe muscular hyperthermia: SEDATE, PARALYSE and INTUBATE. Antipyretics do not work — the heat is being generated by muscle, not by the hypothalamus, so paracetamol is useless. Physical restraints make it worse (isometric contraction against a restraint is exercise). Use a NON-depolarising paralytic (rocuronium) — never succinylcholine, which will dump potassium into an already hyperkalemic patient and can trigger MH.

Exertional heat stroke is different again: cooling is the only thing that matters and minutes count. Cold-water immersion is the gold standard (~0.2 °C/min). Target core temperature <39 °C within 30 minutes, then stop active cooling at ~38 °C to avoid overshoot. Dantrolene does NOT work in heat stroke.

💉

Propofol infusion syndrome — the rhabdomyolysis you cause yourself

Classic risk profile: propofol >4 mg/kg/h for >48 h — but PRIS can occur below that; the thresholds are a risk gradient, not a safe harbour. Additional risk: high catecholamine and glucocorticoid exposure, critical illness, low carbohydrate intake, young age, head injury.

Definition: acute refractory bradycardia progressing to asystole, plus ≥1 of — metabolic acidosis (base deficit >10 mmol/L), rhabdomyolysis, hyperlipidaemia, or an enlarged/fatty liver.

The diagnostic trap: the "textbook" features (hypertriglyceridaemia, fever, hepatomegaly, heart failure) are absent in >95% of cases. You cannot wait for the full picture. The early warning sign is an unexplained, worsening metabolic acidosis with a rising lactate in a patient on prolonged propofol. The ECG changes — ventricular ectopy, then bradycardia with a junctional rhythm — follow hours later. Once the bradycardia appears, you are already late.

Management: STOP THE PROPOFOL IMMEDIATELY, switch sedation, and support — haemodynamic support, haemodialysis, and ECMO for refractory cardiovascular collapse. There is no antidote. Consider screening with a CK in any patient on prolonged or high-dose propofol.

Support

Pressors & haemodynamic support

💉

Which drip — and why fluid comes first

Shock in rhabdomyolysis is HYPOVOLEMIC until proven otherwise. Litres of extracellular fluid have third-spaced into injured muscle. The treatment for that is volume, not vasoconstriction — and reaching for a pressor in an under-resuscitated patient actively worsens renal perfusion by squeezing an already-vasoconstricted afferent arteriole. Fill the tank first. Use a pressor only when the patient remains hypotensive despite adequate volume, or when you must maintain perfusion pressure while you catch up.

Target MAP ≥65 mmHg (higher if chronically hypertensive) — and remember the perfusion pressure you restore is also the diastolic pressure in the delta-pressure calculation, so it directly affects the compartment.

Two rhabdomyolysis-specific pressor points. (1) Restoring the diastolic BP raises the delta pressure — treating the shock genuinely helps the threatened compartment, and a hypotensive patient can hit a critical delta pressure at a deceptively low compartment pressure. (2) β-agonists (epinephrine, and the albuterol you gave for hyperkalemia) shift potassium into cells — so a falling potassium in a patient on an adrenaline infusion may be pharmacological, not real. Do not mistake it for resolution of the disease.
Airway

Respiratory support & ventilation

🌬️

Why a rhabdomyolysis patient ends up intubated

Rhabdomyolysis is not primarily a lung disease. Patients get intubated for four reasons, and it is worth knowing which one you are treating:

  1. Volume overload / pulmonary oedema — often iatrogenic, from resuscitating an oliguric kidney. Treat with RRT and ultrafiltration, not more fluid.
  2. Airway protection — the obtunded, intoxicated, post-ictal or post-arrest patient who caused their rhabdomyolysis by lying still for hours.
  3. To sedate and paralyse a hyperthermic, rigid patient — in MH, NMS or serotonin syndrome, paralysis is the antipyretic therapy, because the muscle is the furnace.
  4. The underlying trigger — sepsis, trauma, ARDS, chest crush injury.

Two intubation traps specific to this disease.

(1) NEVER use succinylcholine. It depolarises muscle and causes a potassium efflux — in a patient whose potassium is already 6.2 from necrotic muscle, that can be immediately fatal. It also triggers malignant hyperthermia in susceptible patients. Use rocuronium (or another non-depolarising agent) for rapid-sequence intubation.

(2) Do not paralyse away a compensatory respiratory alkalosis. A severely acidotic rhabdomyolysis patient is hyperventilating for a reason. If you intubate them and set a comfortable, "normal" minute ventilation, their pH will fall off a cliff and they can arrest peri-intubation. Match — or exceed — their pre-intubation minute ventilation, and check a gas immediately.

Downgrade

When to downgrade

⬇️

De-escalation to the floor

Ready for the floor when ALL of:
  • Potassium <5.5 mEq/L and stable for ≥24 h WITHOUT shifting therapy (no insulin/dextrose, no albuterol) — and the ECG has normalised.
  • CK is clearly and consistently falling — which means muscle has stopped dying, which means the potassium will stop being reloaded.
  • Renal function stable or improving; either not on RRT, or established on a stable, scheduled intermittent regimen the floor can manage.
  • Off vasopressors for ≥12–24 h, haemodynamically stable.
  • Euvolemic — no ongoing high-rate fluid requirement, no pulmonary oedema, able to maintain urine output.
  • Compartment syndrome excluded or definitively treated, with a surgical plan for any fasciotomy wound.
  • The trigger is switched off — normothermic, not seizing, not agitated, offending drug stopped.
  • Extubated (or a stable, planned long-term airway) and acid–base normalised.
NOT yet if…
  • The potassium is "normal" but is being held there by q6h insulin. That is a treated potassium, not a stable one — and the moment the floor's dosing interval stretches, it will climb. This is the classic premature downgrade in rhabdomyolysis.
  • The CK is still rising or has plateaued. Muscle is still dying. Find out why — look at the limbs — before you move them anywhere.
  • They are still needing large-volume fluid, or their urine output only holds up on a high infusion rate.
  • Any limb you are still worried about, or a compartment pressure you have not resolved.
  • Rising creatinine, worsening acidosis, or an unstable dialysis requirement.
The prognosis is genuinely good — say so. Most patients with rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI, including most of those who needed dialysis, recover independent renal function. The disease's mortality is driven overwhelmingly by what caused it (crush, sepsis, multi-organ failure) rather than by the muscle injury itself. A young patient with exertional rhabdomyolysis and a CK of 100,000 will almost certainly walk out. Tell them — and then make sure they leave with the return-to-exercise plan.
