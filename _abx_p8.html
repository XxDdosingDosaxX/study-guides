When outpatient management is safe

🏡

The clinical case for staying home — and the biomarker case

Two independent questions decide disposition, and you must answer both. Clinically: can this patient's physiology tolerate the infection, can they take and absorb an oral drug, and is the source controlled? Biochemically: does the trajectory support de-escalation or stopping? A reassuring biomarker never overrides an unwell patient, and a normal-looking patient with an undrained abscess is not safe at home no matter what the labs say.

CURB-65 is weighted heavily toward age and will under-call severity in a young patient with a high oxygen requirement. The ATS/IDSA guideline prefers the PSI/PORT score for the outpatient-versus-inpatient decision because it is better validated for identifying low-risk patients — it is a 20-variable weighted model, so use a calculator rather than estimating it: PSI/PORT on MDCalc. Neither score assesses hypoxia adequately, and neither accounts for social factors: an unhoused patient, someone unable to obtain the medication, or someone with no one to check on them may need admission with a CURB-65 of 0.

Procalcitonin — using it honestly

Procalcitonin is a precursor of calcitonin released by parenchymal tissues throughout the body in response to bacterial endotoxin and the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6. Interferon-γ, released in viral infection, actively suppresses it — which is the mechanism behind its bacterial-versus-viral discrimination. It begins rising at 3–4 hours, peaks at 6–24 hours, and falls with a half-life of roughly 24 hours, making it useful for tracking a trajectory.

PCT (ng/mL)Interpretation in lower respiratory tract infectionAction
<0.10Bacterial infection very unlikelyStrongly discourage antibiotics
0.10–0.25Bacterial infection unlikelyDiscourage antibiotics
0.25–0.50Bacterial infection possibleEncourage antibiotics
>0.50Bacterial infection likelyStrongly encourage antibiotics
The stopping rule. Therapy is discontinued when procalcitonin falls below 0.25 ng/mL (the respiratory-infection algorithms) or below 0.5 ng/mL (the ICU algorithms), or drops by at least 80% from its peak. Applied inside a protocol this works: ProHOSP cut mean antibiotic exposure from 8.7 to 5.7 days with fewer antibiotic adverse effects (19.8% vs 28.1%) and no excess mortality; PRORATA gained 2.7 antibiotic-free days in the ICU; SAPS shortened therapy from 7 to 5 days. But ProACT — 1,656 patients across 14 US hospitals — found no difference at all (4.2 versus 4.3 antibiotic-days), because US baseline durations were already short and clinicians simply overrode the result. Procalcitonin removes excess duration; where duration is already appropriate it adds nothing, and it only ever helps if you act on it.
Where procalcitonin lies to you.
  • Falsely HIGH without bacterial infection: renal failure and dialysis (reduced clearance), major surgery, trauma, burns, cardiac arrest and post-resuscitation, cardiogenic shock, pancreatitis, medullary thyroid carcinoma and small-cell lung cancer, the first 48 h of life, anti-thymocyte globulin and IL-2 therapy, severe malaria, heat stroke.
  • Falsely LOW despite real bacterial infection: localised, walled-off infection — abscess, empyema, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, mediastinitis; early infection before the 3–4 hour rise; and infection in a profoundly immunosuppressed host.
  • Never useful for: deciding whether an abscess needs drainage, diagnosing C. difficile, or distinguishing colonisation from infection.
A normal procalcitonin in a patient with a drainable collection is a trap. PCT supports a decision; it never makes one.

CRP and ESR are far less specific — CRP rises in any inflammatory state, peaks at 36–50 hours, and has a half-life of ~19 hours; ESR changes over days to weeks and is more useful for following osteomyelitis or endocarditis than for acute decisions. Neither distinguishes bacterial from non-bacterial inflammation reliably enough to start or stop antibiotics on its own.

Evidence-based durations — shorter is almost always right

The single biggest change in antibiotic practice over the last two decades is that nearly every course we were taught is longer than it needs to be. Longer courses do not prevent relapse; they select resistance and cause harm.

"Finish the whole course" is outdated advice for most infections. The idea that stopping early breeds resistance was never supported by evidence; the opposite is true — every extra day of exposure selects resistant organisms in the patient's own flora. The modern instruction is: take it exactly as prescribed for the duration prescribed, and the duration prescribed should be the shortest one the evidence supports.

Ambulatory stewardship is mostly a communication skill. The interventions with the best evidence are not clinical: naming a specific diagnosis ("this is a viral bronchitis"), giving a symptom timeline so the patient knows what normal recovery looks like, offering a delayed prescription, providing a written care plan, and — for clinicians — public commitment posters and peer-comparison feedback on prescribing rates. Antibiotic expectations are far more often assumed by the clinician than actually held by the patient.
Escalate

Red flags — send to the ED

🚑

Do not manage these in clinic

Physiology: SBP <90 or a drop >40 from baseline · HR >120 · RR ≥22 with any confusion (qSOFA ≥2) · SpO₂ <90% on room air · new confusion or reduced consciousness · temperature <36 °C (hypothermia is more ominous than fever) · lactate ≥2 mmol/L if available · mottling or prolonged capillary refill.
Syndromes that need the ED now, not an oral prescription:
  • Necrotising soft-tissue infection — pain out of proportion, rapid progression, bullae, crepitus, skin anaesthesia, dusky skin, systemic toxicity. Surgical emergency.
  • Meningitis — fever with headache, neck stiffness, photophobia or altered mentation. Antibiotics and dexamethasone must not wait for the LP or CT.
  • Febrile neutropenia — any fever in a patient on chemotherapy within the last 6 weeks. Antibiotics within 1 hour.
  • Obstructed infected urinary tract — pyelonephritis with a known stone, hydronephrosis, or anuria. Needs decompression, not just antibiotics.
  • Ascending cholangitis — fever, jaundice and RUQ pain (Charcot's triad), with hypotension and confusion (Reynolds' pentad) marking severe disease. Needs urgent biliary drainage.
  • Septic arthritis — an acutely hot, swollen, exquisitely painful joint. Needs aspiration before antibiotics and often washout.
  • Endocarditis — persistent fever with a new murmur, embolic phenomena, injection drug use, or prosthetic valve/device.
  • Orbital or periorbital cellulitis with proptosis, pain on eye movement, or vision change; Ludwig's angina or any deep neck-space infection with trismus, drooling or voice change — both are airway emergencies.
  • Fournier's gangrene — perineal pain, swelling or crepitus, particularly in a diabetic patient.
Failure of outpatient therapy — no improvement or worsening after 48–72 h of appropriate oral antibiotics — warrants re-evaluation in person, not a phoned-in broader prescription. Before you escalate the drug, escalate the thinking: is there an undrained collection, a resistant organism, a non-infectious mimic, an absorption failure, or an adherence problem?
