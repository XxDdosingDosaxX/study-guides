Admit

The first hour

🚨

What has to happen, and in what order

The 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance organises cardiogenic shock care into a 1-hour and a 24-hour roadmap, and its central recommendation is not a drug or a device — it's that a standardised, interdisciplinary team-based approach is associated with improved outcomes. Community centres are advised to designate an on-site "shock champion" and partner formally with an advanced HF centre. Get the sequence right and the rest of the admission follows.

The shock team is the intervention with the best cost-benefit ratio in this entire guide. No drug in cardiogenic shock has an unequivocal mortality benefit; one device has a positive trial in a narrow population. But standardised team-based care is what the 2025 ACC guidance leads with — because the failures here are usually failures of timing and coordination, not of pharmacology. Call the team early. Say the SCAI stage out loud.

Support

Pressors & inotropes

💉

Which drip, why, and the dose

norepinephrine first, then an inotrope
every one of these drugs converges on intracellular calcium in myocytes or intracellular calcium in vascular smooth muscle. β₁ and PDE3 agents raise myocyte cAMP → PKA → more Ca²⁺ per beat → contractility. α₁ and V₁ agents raise vascular smooth-muscle Ca²⁺ → vasoconstriction → SVR → MAP. Once you know which of those two you're trying to move, the drug choice is almost automatic.

Receptor activity at a glance

Two useful reads. Milrinone is the only agent that touches nothing on the receptor row — it acts downstream of the β-receptor, which is exactly why it still works in a β-blocked or receptor-downregulated heart. And norepinephrine's β₁ activity is real but modest (and mostly lost above ~0.2 mcg/kg/min) — it is a pressure drug, not a flow drug. That's why the classic combination is norepinephrine plus dobutamine.

The default combination is norepinephrine + dobutamine, and the logic is simply that they fix different problems: norepinephrine buys perfusion pressure (and with it, coronary flow), dobutamine buys flow. Titrate each to its own target — norepinephrine to MAP ≥65; dobutamine to lactate/UOP/CI, never to a blood pressure. Titrating dobutamine to MAP is a classic error: it's a vasodilator at the β₂ receptor, so chasing a pressure with it makes the pressure worse.
Every catecholamine has an unavoidable cost. They all increase myocardial oxygen consumption and arrhythmogenicity in a heart that is ischaemic and irritable — which means the drug that raises your cardiac output today is also feeding Loop 1 and Loop 3. There is no vasopressor or inotrope with a proven mortality benefit in cardiogenic shock; observational data consistently link higher cumulative catecholamine exposure to worse outcomes. That is the entire intellectual case for mechanical support: MCS provides flow without the metabolic tax. Use the lowest dose for the shortest time, and treat escalating requirements as a reason to change strategy, not as a reason to keep turning the dial.
Airway

Respiratory support & the ventilator

🌬️

Positive pressure is a cardiac drug — use it deliberately

this is the part people under-appreciate. Positive intrathoracic pressure does three haemodynamically useful things at once in left-sided failure: it reduces preload (less venous return), reduces LV afterload (raising intrathoracic pressure lowers transmural LV wall stress — the ventricle no longer has to eject against negative surrounding pressure), and it abolishes the work of breathing, which in a distressed patient can consume 20% or more of a cardiac output they cannot spare. In LV failure with pulmonary oedema, PEEP is therapy. But the same physiology is dangerous in RV failure and in preload-dependent shock, where reducing venous return is exactly what you must not do.
ModalityWhenWhy / caution
Supplemental O₂Only if SpO₂ <90%Target 92–96%. Do not give O₂ to a non-hypoxic patient — hyperoxia causes coronary vasoconstriction and increased infarct size (AVOID, DETO2X-AMI). Reflexive oxygen is an intervention with a known harm.
NIV — CPAP or BiPAPFirst-line for cardiogenic pulmonary oedema in an awake, protecting patientReduces intubation rates and improves symptoms faster than oxygen alone (3CPO). Start CPAP 8–10 cmH₂O, FiO₂ to target. Buys time for the diuretic and vasodilator to work. Caution: it drops preload — watch for hypotension, especially in RV infarct.
HFNCHypoxaemia without much work of breathing; NIV not toleratedComfortable, some PEEP effect, but less afterload reduction than true NIV. Reasonable bridge or step-down.

Intubating a shock patient can arrest them. Three mechanisms stack at once: (1) sedatives ablate the sympathetic tone that is the only reason the blood pressure exists; (2) positive pressure abruptly cuts venous return; (3) the peri-intubation apnoea worsens acidosis, which blunts catecholamine response. Mitigate deliberately: have the pressor running and turned up before you push drugs (not after), pick a haemodynamically neutral induction agent (etomidate, or ketamine acknowledging its direct myocardial depression in catecholamine-depleted patients), halve your usual sedative dose, pre-oxygenate well, have push-dose pressor drawn up, and have your most experienced operator do it first-pass. Get the arterial line in first if you possibly can.

In RV-predominant shock, flip your instincts. The RV cannot tolerate high afterload, and PEEP + high plateau pressures raise pulmonary vascular resistance directly. Fix

Revascularisation — the only therapy that changes the disease

🫁

Open the culprit — and only the culprit

In AMI-CS this is the intervention with the largest and most durable effect in the entire field, and it rests on two trials that between them tell you both what to do and what not to do.

TrialWhat it testedResultWhat it means at the bedside
SHOCK
NEJM 1999, n=302		Emergency revascularisation (PCI or CABG) vs initial medical stabilisation, within 36 h of MI30 days: 46.7% vs 56.0%, RR 0.83 (0.67–1.04), p=0.11 — not significant. Opening everything at once increases death and renal failure — more contrast, longer procedure, more no-reflow, more distal embolisation in a patient with no reserve. Fix the artery that's killing them. Leave the rest for another day.
Practical rules for the cath lab in shock:
  • Angiography is indicated regardless of time from symptom onset, and regardless of STEMI vs NSTEMI. The 12-hour window does not apply to shock — the artery is the disease.
  • PCI the culprit only. Stage the rest. (Exception: a genuinely ambiguous culprit, or persistent shock clearly attributable to a second critical lesion.)
  • Left main or complex triple-vessel disease → consider emergency CABG, with MCS as a bridge to the OR.
  • Fibrinolysis is a poor substitute — reperfusion rates are much lower in a low-pressure, low-flow system. Use it only if PCI is genuinely unavailable within a reasonable window, then transfer for angiography.
  • Oral P2Y12 absorption is unreliable in shock (gut hypoperfusion, opiates, vomiting) — consider IV cangrelor or a GPIIb/IIIa inhibitor peri-procedurally.
  • Do not withhold angiography over contrast nephropathy fear. The kidneys are being injured by the shock far more than by the dye; the fastest renal protection available is fixing the output.
The intra-aortic balloon pump. For decades the reflex device in AMI-CS — inflates in diastole to augment coronary perfusion, deflates in systole to reduce afterload. Physiologically sensible; it only augments cardiac output by roughly 0.5 L/min. IABP-SHOCK II tested the reflex and it failed (below), which is why it has been de-implemented from routine use.

Mechanical circulatory support

⚙️

When drugs aren't enough — and what the trials actually showed

MCS is the most conceptually attractive idea in cardiogenic shock: flow without the metabolic tax of catecholamines, plus mechanical unloading of the ventricle. It is also the area where enthusiasm has most consistently outrun the evidence. Three big randomised trials define what we actually know.

DeviceFlowWhat it doesEvidenceWhere it stands
IABP~0.5 L/min Counterpulsation: inflates in diastole (↑coronary perfusion), deflates in systole (↓afterload). Needs a native rhythm and native output to time against. Why did DanGer Shock succeed where IABP-SHOCK II and ECLS-SHOCK failed? The most credible explanation is that it's the only one of the three that delivered meaningful flow plus genuine LV unloading to a narrowly selected population, early. IABP adds ~0.5 L/min — physiologically pretty but haemodynamically trivial. VA-ECMO adds enormous flow but raises LV afterload and doesn't unload unless you vent it, so it fights Loop 1 with one hand while feeding it with the other. The microaxial pump is the one device that raises flow and drops LVEDP at the same time. That mechanism is worth understanding, because it predicts where these devices help and where they don't.
When to escalate to MCS — no randomised threshold exists, so these are the criteria in common use rather than guideline-mandated numbers:
  • SCAI Stage D or E — failure of an adequate trial of initial pharmacological support
  • Rising lactate despite two vasoactive agents
  • CPO <0.6 W despite pharmacological support
  • Escalating catecholamine requirement — the dose trajectory matters more than the number
  • AMI-CS with STEMI — the DanGer Shock population; the evidence favours early insertion, not rescue after everything else has failed
  • Bridge to a specific decision — to the OR (mechanical complications), to transplant, to durable LVAD, or to recovery (fulminant myocarditis)
  • Refractory arrest with a reversible cause and good pre-arrest function → eCPR
And the harder question — when NOT to: irreversible neurological injury, multi-organ failure without a recovery path, no candidacy for transplant/LVAD and no realistic recovery, or goals of care that don't align. SCAI is unusually direct about this: transfers for futile care in unrecoverable high-risk patients do not change outcomes and deny capacity to those who might otherwise benefit. Deciding the destination before the device is the single most important thing you can do here — a device with no destination is just a slower death with more tubes.

IABP-SHOCK II risk score — 30-day mortality in AMI-CS

The best-validated mortality score specific to acute-MI-related cardiogenic shock. Six variables, all available shortly after PCI, giving a >3-fold mortality gradient. Use it for prognostic conversations, triage, and to calibrate how aggressive escalation should be — not to decide who to treat.

Age >73 years +1
Prior stroke +2
Glucose on admission >191 mg/dL (>10.6 mmol/L) +1
Creatinine on admission >1.5 mg/dL (>132.6 µmol/L) +1
Arterial lactate on admission >5 mmol/L +2
TIMI flow grade <3 after PCI +2
0
SourcePoints and thresholds per the derivation by Pöss et al., JACC 2017 (from the IABP-SHOCK II trial and registry; AUC 0.79). 30-day mortality by tier — low (0–2) 23.8%, intermediate (3–4) 49.2%, high (5–9) 76.6% — is from that derivation cohort. External validation in the North American Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network registry reproduced the gradient in AMI-CS (26.5% / 52.2% / 77.5% in-hospital mortality) but performed less well in non-AMI cardiogenic shock — so do not apply it to your HF-CS patient. Maximum 9 points (7 if no PCI/TIMI data).
Notice what the score is made of: lactate and TIMI flow carry 2 points each — i.e. "how badly are you perfusing" and "did we actually open the artery." Those are the two things that matter most, and they're the two things you can still change. Age, prior stroke, glucose and creatinine are the fixed background. A high score should sharpen the goals-of-care conversation; it should never be the reason not to open an artery.

Weaning & when to step down

⬇️

De-escalation criteria — and the "not yet" list

The weaning order

De-escalate in the reverse order of escalation, one variable at a time, with a reassessment between each step. Never wean two supports at once — when the patient deteriorates you won't know which change caused it.

  1. Wean the vasopressor first (norepinephrine down as the MAP tolerates), because it's the one adding afterload the ventricle is paying for.
  2. Then the device. IABP: reduce augmentation 1:1 → 1:2 → 1:3 over hours, watching the haemodynamics at each ratio. Impella: step the P-level down and watch for a rising PCWP or falling CPO before removal.
  3. Then the inotrope, slowly — dobutamine down in 2.5 mcg/kg/min steps, milrinone in 0.125 steps, allowing hours between reductions (and remembering milrinone's long tail in renal impairment).
  4. Then vasopressin — conventionally weaned after the norepinephrine, since abrupt discontinuation can cause a sharp pressure drop.
  5. Then start GDMT, at low doses, once they're genuinely off inotropes — and titrate as an inpatient rather than leaving it to clinic (STRONG-HF).

Not yet — hold the downgrade if ANY of these are true, even if the blood pressure looks fine:
  • Weaned off pressors less than 12 hours ago — the classic bounce-back. The floor is not where you want to discover they can't hold their own pressure.
  • Lactate normal but only just, or normalised in the last few hours — you want it durably normal.
  • Still requiring any inotrope — that's a SCAI Stage C patient. It doesn't matter that the dose is small.
  • Creatinine still rising, even if the urine output looks acceptable — the kidney is telling you the perfusion isn't fixed.
  • Active ischaemia, incomplete revascularisation, or a staged PCI still pending
  • New or worsening arrhythmia within 24 h
  • An unaddressed mechanical lesion — severe MR, a VSD, critical AS awaiting intervention
  • Rising or plateaued NT-proBNP with a rising weight — re-accumulating congestion
  • No destination decided. If nobody can say what the plan is, the patient is not stable — the plan is unstable, and it will fail at 3am on a floor without the people who understand it.
The most common premature downgrade is the patient who came off norepinephrine at 6am and moves to the floor at 2pm looking well. Their pressure is "fine" because they are lying flat, sedated-adjacent, and not doing anything. The first time they sit up, walk to the toilet, or get a dose of their restarted β-blocker, they declare that their reserve is still gone. Give them a full day off support, and watch them do something — sit up, ambulate, eat — before you move them.
ICU disposition isn't only "the floor." The honest options after cardiogenic shock also include: transfer to a shock/transplant hub if they're MCS- or transplant-eligible and you're a spoke; LTAC for the patient who survived but needs prolonged vent weaning; and palliative care/hospice for refractory shock, non-candidacy, or goals that favour comfort — including palliative inotropes at home, which are a legitimate symptom therapy and not an admission of defeat. Make this decision with the patient or their surrogate, and make it before it makes itself.
