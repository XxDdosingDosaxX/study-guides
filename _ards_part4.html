🛏️ Wards — the oxygen ladder, and knowing when it has failed
Admit

Who gets admitted — and who goes straight to the unit

📋

Admission criteria & level of care

Level of careCriteria
HomeSpO₂ ≥ 92% on room air at rest and on ambulation, RR < 20, no work of breathing, a treatable cause with reliable outpatient follow-up, and a patient who can get back to hospital quickly.
Ward / telemetryNew oxygen requirement that is modest and stable — typically ≤ 4–6 L/min nasal cannula to hold SpO₂ ≥ 92% (≥ 88% in chronic CO₂ retainers). RR < 25. Normal mentation. Haemodynamically stable. No acidosis. A cause you can treat on a ward.
Step-down / intermediate careNeeds HFNC or NIV, or oxygen requirement that is climbing, but is otherwise stable — if your institution allows these outside the ICU. Many do not, and that is a reasonable policy: the patient who needs HFNC is the patient who may need intubating.
ICU — go straight thereAny of: P/F ≤ 300 with bilateral infiltrates (i.e. ARDS by definition); FiO₂ requirement > 50% or ≥ 6 L/min to maintain saturation; RR ≥ 30; accessory-muscle use or paradoxical breathing; pH < 7.30 with a rising PaCO₂; altered mental status; haemodynamic instability or a rising lactate; or any patient you think might need intubating in the next few hours.
If the patient meets the Berlin criteria, they have ARDS, and ARDS is an ICU diagnosis. There is no such thing as "mild ARDS we'll watch on the floor." Even Berlin-mild ARDS carries roughly 27–35% mortality — worse than most STEMIs. The floor cannot deliver lung-protective ventilation, cannot prone, and cannot rescue a patient who deteriorates in twenty minutes. Make the diagnosis explicitly, say the word "ARDS" out loud, and move the patient. The most consequential failure in this disease — quantified by LUNG SAFE, which found ARDS was recognised in only ~60% of cases — is not calling it.

FiO₂Whatever holds the targetWean first, before you wean pressureSpO₂ 92–96% — or 88–92% in chronic CO₂ retainersOxygen is the easiest thing to give and the least likely to be the answer. Wean it early so it does not mask a deteriorating pump.
Above IPAP ~25 cmH₂O you exceed the lower oesophageal sphincter pressure and start insufflating the stomach — which causes vomiting, aspiration, and a splinted diaphragm. If you need pressures that high to ventilate the patient, the patient needs a tube, not a bigger number.
Contraindications to NIV — the patient who needs a tube, now: cardiac or respiratory arrest; inability to protect the airway or an impaired conscious level (with one important exception — hypercapnic COPD encephalopathy often improves rapidly on NIV and is not an automatic bar); active vomiting or upper GI bleeding; facial trauma, burns or surgery preventing a seal; undrained pneumothorax; copious secretions the patient cannot clear; haemodynamic instability or life-threatening arrhythmia; bowel obstruction; and an agitated, uncooperative patient. And the big one: NIV is a treatment for a problem you expect to fix in hours. It is not a way to postpone an inevitable intubation.

🧮

Calculator — ROX index (is HFNC working?)

The danger of HFNC is that it makes a failing patient look comfortable. They are warm, humidified, talking to you — and quietly heading for a crash intubation. The ROX index is the objective check. It combines the two things that actually track with failure: how well they are oxygenating (SpO₂/FiO₂) and how hard they are working to do it (respiratory rate). A patient maintaining their saturation only by breathing 40 times a minute has a low ROX and is failing, however calm they look.

ROX = (SpO₂ / FiO₂) / respiratory rate. Thresholds from Roca O, et al. Am J Respir Crit Care Med 2019;199(11):1368–1376 — a ROX ≥ 4.88 measured at 2, 6 or 12 h predicts HFNC success; the high-risk-of-failure thresholds are < 2.85 at 2 h, < 3.47 at 6 h, and < 3.85 at 12 h. Between the two lies a genuine grey zone where clinical judgement governs. A ROX that is falling between time points is worrying regardless of its absolute value.

Treat

Treating the cause — the ladder, with real doses

💊

Stepwise inpatient therapy

There is no drug for ARDS. Read that again, because it reorganises everything you do. The only therapies that reduce mortality in ARDS are mechanical (low tidal volume, prone positioning), a fluid strategy, and — in selected patients — corticosteroids. Everything else on this page is aimed at the precipitant. So the single most important therapeutic act on the ward is to find and treat the cause: the pneumonia, the sepsis, the aspiration, the pancreatitis. Chasing the PaO₂ while the source goes untreated is the classic way to lose one of these patients.
1
Oxygen to target, and treat the precipitant
Antibiotics within the hour if sepsis is on the table.
▾

Oxygen targets: SpO₂ 92–96% for most patients; 88–92% if there is any suspicion of chronic CO₂ retention. Do not aim higher — hyperoxia causes absorption atelectasis and oxidative injury, and liberal oxygen targets have been associated with harm.

2
Fluid strategy — resuscitate, then stop, then remove
The three phases, and the discipline to switch between them.
▾

This is where wards most often do harm, because the two phases require opposite behaviour and nobody announces the transition.

PhaseWhat to doWhy
1. Resuscitation
(first hours, if shocked)		30 mL/kg crystalloid for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion (Surviving Sepsis), then reassess with dynamic measures — passive leg raise, stroke-volume variation, or an echo — not with a static CVP.Perfusion first. A dead patient has an excellent fluid balance.
2. Stabilisation → de-resuscitation
(once shock has resolved)		STOP the fluids. Then aim for a net-negative balance. This is the FACTT strategy (NEJM 2006): the conservative arm achieved a roughly even 7-day fluid balance (versus +7 L in the liberal arm) and gained ~2.5 more ventilator-free days (14.6 vs 12.1) — with no increase in shock or in dialysis.The alveolar–capillary barrier is a sieve. Starling's forces still operate through a sieve — they just move far more fluid, and it is protein-rich. You cannot diurese away a leak, but you can stop driving fluid through it. Every extra litre of positive balance widens the flood.
3. OngoingDaily weights, strict I/O, and a daily explicit fluid-balance target written in the plan.If it is not written down, it will not happen. "Even to negative today" is a real order.
The FACTT conservative strategy applies only to the patient who is OUT of shock. Do not diurese a patient who is still on rising pressors with a lactate of 6 — you will make the shock worse, and the trial explicitly excluded that patient. The order is: resuscitate → stabilise → de-resuscitate. The error is doing them in the wrong order, or never getting to the third.

Diuretic resistance: work through the list rather than just giving more. (1) Is the dose big enough to reach the threshold? Loops have a sigmoid dose–response — a sub-threshold dose does nothing, so doubling beats repeating. (2) Is it getting there? Gut oedema destroys oral absorption — go IV. (3) Is there distal escape? Add a thiazide. (4) Is the patient hypochloraemic or alkalotic? Loops need chloride to work — replace it. (5) Is the albumin very low? Furosemide is protein-bound for delivery to the tubule. (6) Are NSAIDs on the chart? They block the prostaglandin-mediated renal vasodilation the diuretic depends on. (7) Is the patient actually still in shock? — in which case, stop.

Do NOT start steroids in LATE ARDS. The ARDSNet LaSRS trial (NEJM 2006) found that methylprednisolone started after 14 days of ARDS was associated with increased 60-day mortality. It also found more neuromuscular weakness. Steroids in ARDS are an early-phase drug. If you are meeting the patient on day 16, that window has closed. And in every case: rule out an untreated or inadequately drained infection first — steroids on top of an undrained empyema or an uncontrolled source is how you kill someone.

Knowing what to stop ordering is as valuable as knowing what to order.

Do use: bronchodilators (albuterol 2.5 mg + ipratropium 0.5 mg nebulised q4–6h) if there is a genuine obstructive component — but not reflexively for ARDS, which is not a bronchospastic disease; analgesia, so the patient can take a deep breath and cough; chest physiotherapy and early mobilisation; VTE prophylaxis; and enteral nutrition.

Escalate

When to call the ICU

⬆️

Numeric triggers — no hand-waving

Call the ICU — now — for ANY of the following:
DomainTrigger
OxygenationP/F ≤ 300 with bilateral infiltrates (= ARDS). SpO₂ < 90% despite FiO₂ ≥ 50%, or requiring > 6 L/min nasal cannula / any need for a non-rebreather to stay ≥ 92%. Or a rising FiO₂ requirement over 12–24 h.
HFNC failingROX < 3.85 at 12 h (or < 2.85 at 2 h, < 3.47 at 6 h) — or a ROX that is falling between measurements at all.
NIV failingNo improvement in pH, PaCO₂ or RR after 1–2 h on well-fitted NIV. This is the single most important escalation trigger on the ward, and the one most often missed — a patient left on failing NIV overnight is the classic preventable ICU disaster.
VentilationRR ≥ 35 — or < 8. pH < 7.30 with a rising PaCO₂. Any paradoxical abdominal motion or inability to speak.
HaemodynamicsSBP < 90 or MAP < 65 despite fluids; any need for a vasopressor; lactate > 4 or rising.
NeurologyAny new altered mental status — agitation, confusion, or somnolence. GCS ≤ 8 → intubate.
GestaltYou think they might need intubating in the next few hours. That is a sufficient reason on its own, and it does not require a number.
The most dangerous patient on the ward is the one on HFNC or NIV who "looks comfortable." Both therapies are extremely good at making a failing patient appear well while their respiratory muscles quietly exhaust and, on HFNC, while they generate large injurious tidal volumes on their own (P-SILI). Delayed intubation after failed non-invasive support is independently associated with higher mortality. Do not congratulate yourself for avoiding a tube — set a deadline in advance ("if the ROX is not above 4.88 at 6 hours, we intubate") and hold yourself to it. NIV and HFNC are treatments for a problem you expect to fix in hours. They are not a strategy for postponing an inevitable intubation to a worse hour of the night.
The decision you are really making. Intubation is not a failure of ward management — it is a treatment, and one whose benefit falls the longer you postpone it. The right time to intubate the failing patient is a controlled, planned, daylight procedure with your best airway operator present — not a crash call at 4 a.m. after eight hours of "let's see how they do on BiPAP." The tube is not the bad outcome. The delayed tube is.

Dispo

Discharge & disposition

🏠

Readiness — and where they actually go

Discharge readiness — all of these:

  • SpO₂ ≥ 92% on room air (or on their established home oxygen), including on ambulation — walk them before you discharge them. This single step prevents a large fraction of bounce-backs.
  • RR < 20, no accessory-muscle use, speaking in full sentences.
  • Afebrile ≥ 24 h, haemodynamically stable, and the precipitant is treated with a defined plan to finish the course.
  • Eating and drinking; oral medications tolerated; swallow assessed if there was any intubation or stroke.
  • If newly on oxygen: the equipment is physically delivered and demonstrated, not merely ordered — and a 60–90 day re-assessment is booked to see whether they still qualify (many do not).
  • Follow-up within 7 days, and the patient can state what they are watching for and who to call.

Home

Back to baseline. Independent with ADLs. No oxygen, or already established on home O₂ at a stable flow. Reliable follow-up.

Home health

Home + skilled nursing/therapy visits. The right answer for most new-oxygen and post-pneumonia patients — someone lays eyes on them, checks the equipment actually works, and confirms they are taking the antibiotics.

SNF

Needs 24-h skilled nursing but can tolerate < 3 h of therapy/day. Typical for the deconditioned elderly patient after a long pneumonia admission.

Acute rehab (IRF)

Can tolerate ≥ 3 h of therapy/day across ≥ 2 disciplines, and has the potential to make measurable functional gains. The right destination for the ARDS survivor with ICU-acquired weakness who still has the reserve to work.

LTAC

Prolonged complex care, typically > 25 days — above all, ventilator weaning. The patient with a tracheostomy who could not be liberated in the ICU. This is where the fibrotic-phase ARDS patient goes.

Hospice / palliative

Irreversible respiratory failure, end-stage lung disease, or a patient who does not want further escalation. Involve palliative care EARLY and in parallel with active treatment — not as the thing you do once you have given up.
